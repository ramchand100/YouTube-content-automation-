# CLAUDE.md — Channel Production Context & Editorial Constitution

This file is the single source of truth for every script, storyboard, thumbnail,
and research brief produced in this repository. Any content generated here must
comply with the rules below. When a request conflicts with this file, this file
wins.

---

## 1. The Channel

A high-production, English-language YouTube channel analyzing **Pakistan's
business ecosystem, macroeconomics, corporate strategy, and economic mechanics.**

This is not a generic clone of global case-study channels. It is an insider-driven
media engine that explains the *ground truth* of doing business in Pakistan: the
informal cash economy, Seth management culture, State Bank (SBP) regulation, FBR
tax policy, logistics and energy bottlenecks, import constraints, and local tech
adoption. The edge is authenticity and local mechanics, not surface-level summary.

---

## 2. Language and Voice

### Language

Use strictly professional, direct, engaging English.

Do not use Urdu, Roman Urdu, or Urdu-English hybrid language in voiceover or
on-screen script text.

Local terms with no clean English equivalent may be used only as named analytical
concepts. Define each term in plain English the first time it appears.

Write for an intelligent viewer who knows little about finance or economics.

Use simple words without simplifying the analysis.

Prefer:

- Concrete nouns.
- Active verbs.
- Short, natural sentences.
- Specific examples.
- Visible consequences.
- Plain explanations of technical mechanics.

Avoid:

- Academic language.
- Bureaucratic language.
- Corporate jargon.
- Abstract nouns stacked together.
- Long sentences with several clauses.
- Passive voice where active voice is clearer.
- Statistics without interpretation.
- Conclusions that appear before the evidence.

### Spoken-language standard

The script must sound natural when read aloud by a professional narrator.

It must not sound like:

- A report being read aloud.
- A university lecture.
- A policy memo.
- A newspaper editorial.
- A list of statistics.
- An AI-generated explainer.

Read every paragraph as spoken audio.

If a paragraph sounds like written research rather than spoken analysis, rewrite it.

### Sentence rhythm

Do not make every sentence short.

Use varied rhythm:

- Short sentences for weight.
- Medium sentences for explanation.
- Longer sentences only when connecting a clear chain of cause and effect.

A normal paragraph should not contain a series of dramatic fragments.

Use fragments rarely and deliberately.

Do not repeat the same sentence pattern across paragraphs.

### Analytical clarity

When a technical term is necessary:

1. Name it.
2. Define it in plain English.
3. Show how it works in Pakistan.
4. Explain who pays, gains, or carries the risk.

Do not define a term and then immediately move to another unrelated fact.

Keep the viewer inside the mechanism.

## 3. Target Audience

Primary:
- Pakistani entrepreneurs and startup founders.
- Corporate professionals and mid-to-senior managers.
- University students in business, economics, finance, and engineering.

Secondary:
- Foreign investors and analysts trying to understand the Pakistani market.
- The overseas Pakistani diaspora (Gulf, UK, US, Canada).

They are intelligent and time-poor. They want mechanics and consequences, not
motivational filler. Write up to their intelligence.

## 4. Documentary Storytelling and Tone

The script must be an evidence-based story, not a research summary.

Facts are the evidence. The story is the sequence of decisions, incentives,
failures, adaptations, and consequences that those facts reveal.

### Central narrative principle

Every episode must answer four questions:

1. What changed?
2. Why did it change?
3. Who adapted or benefited?
4. Who carries the cost now?

The script must make the connection between these questions visible. Do not
simply place facts next to one another.

### Story movement

Every major part of the script must do at least one of the following:

- Introduce a concrete problem.
- Challenge an obvious explanation.
- Reveal a hidden mechanism.
- Show a decision or incentive.
- Demonstrate a consequence.
- Introduce a counterargument.
- Narrow or revise the thesis.
- Change the viewer's understanding of the problem.
- Establish what would need to change.

A paragraph that only adds information without moving the argument should be
removed or combined with another paragraph.

### Documentary spine

Before writing, create this internal chain:

```
Concrete anomaly
→ viewer assumption
→ evidence that complicates the assumption
→ mechanism
→ decision or incentive
→ consequence
→ wider economic effect
→ realistic answer
```

The final script does not need to display these labels, but the narration must
follow a clear version of this movement.

### Concrete entry point

Open with a documented event, decision, transaction, failure, price, shutdown,
filing, policy change, or institutional action.

Do not open with a general definition, a list of historical facts, or broad
statements such as:

- "Pakistan has always struggled with..."
- "In today's rapidly changing world..."
- "The economy faces many challenges."
- "This is an important issue."
- "The numbers tell a troubling story."

If a personal scene is not documented, use an institutional or commercial scene:
a port waiting for cargo, a factory choosing between transport options, a company
filing showing a cost shift, a ministry changing an allocation, a regulator
changing a tariff, a railway losing a major freight contract.

Do not invent people, dialogue, private thoughts, or physical scenes.

### Narrative tension

Create tension through competing realities, not hype. Useful tensions include:

- The cheaper option is less reliable.
- The profitable service carries a public obligation.
- The policy helps one group while raising costs for another.
- A reform improves the balance sheet but harms a stakeholder.
- A company grows revenue while losing cash.
- A sector expands while margins shrink.
- A solution exists technically but is blocked institutionally.
- The market appears inefficient until the hidden constraint is revealed.

Do not manufacture suspense. Do not use exaggerated words to create importance.
Let the conflict between incentives and outcomes carry the tension.

### Facts inside arguments

Do not present statistics as isolated objects.

**Weak:**
> Pakistan Railways earned Rs 88.8 billion. Freight contributed Rs 28 billion.
> Passenger services contributed Rs 47 billion.

**Stronger:**
> Pakistan Railways earned Rs 88.8 billion in FY2023-24, but freight contributed
> only Rs 28 billion. Passenger services contributed Rs 47 billion. The revenue
> mix shows what the railway had become: a passenger service carrying a shrinking
> freight business.

The stronger version does not merely report numbers. It tells the viewer what
the numbers mean.

### Human consequence without fictional characters

Human relevance does not require invented characters. Show consequences through:

- Factory costs.
- Delivery delays.
- Food prices.
- Export margins.
- Household bills.
- Employment.
- Working capital.
- Inventory.
- Tax exposure.
- Investment decisions.
- Regional access.
- Business closures.
- Public-service tradeoffs.

Use a real company, location, or industry only when the source supports the claim.

### Institutions as actors

Do not describe institutions as abstract nouns only. Explain what each
institution did or failed to do.

**Weak:** Infrastructure prioritisation created an imbalance.

**Stronger:** Successive budgets expanded road capacity while railway investment
remained too limited to modernise the freight network.

The second version gives the viewer an action, a comparison, and a consequence.

### Explain the system through decisions

Whenever possible, connect a structural result to a decision:

- Who made the decision?
- What problem were they solving?
- What incentive shaped the choice?
- What did the decision improve?
- What cost did it create?
- Who absorbed that cost?
- Was the outcome intended or unintended?

Do not assign motives without evidence. Use "the effect was," "the incentive
was," or "the policy created" when intent cannot be proven.

### Section and part transitions

Do not use headings as the only structure. End each part by creating the next
analytical question. Examples:

- "If roads were more flexible, why did rail lose even bulk cargo?"
- "That explains the customer's decision. It does not explain why the railway
  could not replace the lost business."
- "The revenue problem is visible in the accounts. The operating problem appears
  on the ground."
- "That policy solved an immediate bottleneck. It also changed who controlled
  the freight market."
- "The cost did not disappear when cargo left rail. It moved into the road system."

A transition must move the investigation forward.

### The conclusion

Do not repeat the thesis in different words. The ending must answer:

- What is the most defensible finding?
- What remains uncertain?
- What would need to change?
- What cannot be fixed quickly?
- Who should care?
- What decision follows for founders, companies, investors, professionals, or
  students?

End with a practical implication, not a motivational slogan.

### Visual identity

- **Background:** off-white `#F8F9FA`
- **Primary text:** near-black `#1A1A1A`
- **Secondary text / captions:** mid-grey `#555555`
- **Green accent:** `#1EB53A` — growth, positive path, viable option
- **Red accent:** `#D32F2F` — cost gap, risk, circular debt, decline
- **Card background:** white `#FFFFFF` with subtle shadow
- **Divider / border:** light grey `#E0E0E0`
- **Type:** Inter (body, captions, definitions) · Archivo Black (numbers, titles)
- **Motion graphics:** generated with Remotion (React). Data in JSON per episode.
- **Footage:** assembled in CapCut — editor adds B-roll at FOOTAGE cues from storyboard.

## 5. Formatting & Length Rules

Scripts are **complete, un-cut, full-length production scripts.** No outlines
handed off as if finished, no "[continue here]" placeholders.

- **Length:** default 1,800 to 2,500 words, equal to roughly 12 to 16 minutes of
  voiceover at a documentary pace (~150 words/minute). This is the target for most
  episodes.
- **Length is set by the topic, not by a quota.** A topic that genuinely needs more
  room to be explained properly may run longer, up to roughly 3,500 to 3,800 words
  (about 24 to 26 minutes). Go long only when the extra length is carrying real
  substance: more mechanics, more PKR math, a second case, a necessary piece of
  history. Never pad to hit a number, and never stretch a simple idea. If a topic is
  fully explained in 12 minutes, it ends at 12 minutes. Length must be earned, every
  minute of it. Longer episodes deepen their sections; they do not add filler.
- **Scripts are pure voiceover prose.** No visual cue annotations inside the
  script. All visual direction lives in the companion storyboard file
  (`storyboards/NN_*.md`), which is the single source of truth for editors.
- **Source citations** remain inline in the script as `[SOURCE: publication, year]`
  and are collected in the Sources block at the end.
- Any figure that moves fast or could not be fully confirmed is tagged
  `[VERIFY]` so it is re-checked before recording.

## 6. Editorial Rules

1. **Analyze through Pakistani economic reality.** Always connect a topic to the
   real local machinery: SBP policy rate and monetary stance, informal cash
   markets, the documented-vs-undocumented economy, FBR tax structure and
   withholding regime, DC/FBR property valuations, import LC constraints, energy
   tariffs and circular debt, and regulatory friction. A generic global framing
   that could apply to any country is a failure.
2. **No repetition within a script.** Every concept, number, and definition is
   stated once — at the point where it first matters. If a later section needs to
   reference it, one clause is enough ("those DISCO losses," "the interest burden
   already covered"). Never re-explain something already explained.
3. **No generic corporate clichés.** Ban phrases like "game-changer," "at the end
   of the day," "synergy," and "disrupt" unless used precisely and critically.
4. **Data discipline.** Sources first. If a number cannot be sourced, either drop
   it or clearly label it an estimate. Never invent a statistic to sound credible.
   This rigor is the entire competitive moat; do not spend it cheaply.
5. **Fairness and safety.** Business content will touch politics, powerful groups,
   the state's economic footprint, and named companies and families. Frame around
   documented facts and mechanics, present multiple sides on contested points, and
   never state an unproven allegation as fact. This protects credibility and the
   creator.
6. **Freshness.** Macro figures (SBP rate, inflation, FX, tax rates) change fast.
   Re-verify anything time-sensitive right before recording; do not reuse a stale
   number from an older episode.

## 7. The Investigative Brief Structure (every long-form episode)

The section count is determined by the approved structure, not a template default.
Episodes may have three, four, five, six, or more sections — whatever the story
logic and evidence require. The section count must be proposed, justified, and
approved before writing begins. See Section 8 (Mandatory Pre-writing Stages).

Section names and focus areas adapt to the content type. Three legacy templates
(A, B, C) are defined below as a reference; they remain valid options when the
story logic fits them cleanly.

**Three commitments that never flex:**
1. The opening part always begins with a concrete real-world anomaly in short,
   punchy sentences, and closes with the episode's single central question.
2. The middle parts always show the financial or structural mechanics before the
   conclusion. Never jump from anomaly to opinion.
3. The closing part always delivers a grounded, honest outlook — naming what would
   need to change, what cannot be fixed quickly, and who this matters to.

---

### Opening part — universal

Open on a concrete real-world anomaly. State the stakes plainly, without
manufactured drama.

**Sentence rhythm (non-negotiable):** Use short, punchy sentences to create weight
and pause. Echo a key word or phrase for emphasis. Name the anomaly bluntly before
explaining it. Use a transitional sentence that frames the anomaly as deliberate,
not accidental ("That silence was not an accident." / "That was not a coincidence."
/ "That gap did not appear by itself."). End the opening part by stating — in plain
English — the single question the episode will answer.

Pattern example:

> [Single concrete action, one sentence.] [What they needed, why it mattered.]
> [Result — blunt.] [Echo for weight: "Zero bids. No counter-offers. Just silence."]
>
> [That X was not an accident. Brief explanation of the larger force at work.]
>
> [Ground-level consequence — real people, real places, real numbers.]
>
> [Name the trap / the system / the problem in one line.]
>
> [Closing line: "That [bill / gap / silence], and why it keeps growing, is what
> this episode is about."]

---

### Closing part — universal

A realistic, grounded finding. No manufactured optimism or pessimism. Name
specifically what would need to change for the situation to improve, what the
concrete obstacles are, and who this matters to — founders, executives, investors,
or students — depending on the episode's subject.

---

### Legacy templates for middle sections

The three legacy templates define section names for middle sections. They remain
valid when the story logic fits them cleanly. Template choice follows the central
question, not the subject category:

- "How does this cost or earn money?" — Template A or B.
- "Why does this exist / how did we get here?" — Template C.

---

**Template A — Macro / Institutions**
*Use for: government policy, SOEs, energy, monetary and fiscal topics, regulatory
structures, trade policy, sector-wide failures.*

| Section | Name | Focus |
|---------|------|-------|
| 2 | The Paper Trail | PKR unit economics, government budgets, subsidy flows, cost-margin anatomy in rupees |
| 3 | The Field Reality | How the system runs day to day; informal networks, the cash layer, middleman dynamics the numbers alone don't show |
| 4 | The Systemic Domino Effect | How this one system ripples into the wider economy; SBP / FBR / FX / energy connections |

---

**Template B — Company Case Study**
*Use for: named companies, corporate strategy, industry deep-dives, listed or
private firms, sector leaders and failures.*

| Section | Name | Focus |
|---------|------|-------|
| 2 | The Business Model | Revenue model, unit economics, margin structure — how this company actually makes or loses money |
| 3 | The Operational Reality | Supply chain, workforce, compliance, the informal layer, what actually makes the operation run |
| 4 | The Competitive Position | Market position vs. regional and global peers; FX, energy, and regulatory exposure; what threatens the model |

---

**Template C — Structural Dependency / History**
*Use for: long-cycle structural stories (remittances, migration, debt cycles, land),
historical investigations ("how did we get here"), and policy decisions whose
consequences play out over decades.*

| Section | Name | Focus |
|---------|------|-------|
| 2 | The Origin | How and when the structure was built; the decision, policy, or event that created it |
| 3 | How It Plays Out | Ground-level consequences — households, towns, industries — that the origin story produced |
| 4 | The Structural Risk | What threatens the model, what has already begun to change, and what the numbers say |

## 8. Mandatory Pre-writing Stages

Complete these stages in order. Do not begin full script drafting until Stage 3
is approved.

### Stage 1 — Story design

Before writing the script, produce a story design document covering:

```markdown
# Story Design: [Title]

## Central question
[The single question this episode answers.]

## Viewer's starting assumption
[What will an informed but non-specialist viewer probably assume?]

## What the evidence complicates
[Which part of that assumption is incomplete or wrong?]

## Core mechanism
[Explain the cause-and-effect chain in plain language.]

## Main actors
[List the institutions, companies, industries, workers, consumers, or investors
affected by the mechanism.]

## Incentive conflict
[What does each major actor want, and where do those incentives collide?]

## Concrete consequences
[List the effects on businesses, households, government, investors, or workers.]

## Counterargument
[What is the strongest alternative explanation?]

## Editorial finding
[What can the evidence support confidently?]

## Unresolved questions
[What cannot yet be proven?]

## Narrative progression

### Opening
What documented anomaly begins the investigation?

### Development
What does the viewer need to understand next?

### Complication
What obvious explanation is incomplete?

### Mechanism
How does the system actually work?

### Consequence
Where does the cost or benefit appear?

### Ending
What would need to change, and what remains difficult?
```

Do not begin full script drafting until the story design shows a clear causal,
institutional, or chronological progression.

### Stage 2 — Fact-to-story test

For every major fact, identify its function. A fact must do at least one of the
following:

- Establish the baseline.
- Show a change.
- Prove a mechanism.
- Reveal an incentive.
- Introduce a conflict.
- Demonstrate a consequence.
- Challenge an assumption.
- Support or weaken the thesis.
- Establish uncertainty.
- Set up the next part.

If a fact has no clear function, remove it. Do not include a number merely because
it is available. Do not include a source merely because it is reputable. Every
statistic must change the viewer's understanding or move the argument forward.

### Stage 3 — Structure approval

Propose the number of parts and the name and purpose of each part. Explain why
this structure fits the approved angle and central question. Do not begin writing
until the structure is explicitly approved.

## 9. Pacing and Information Density

Do not place several statistics in consecutive sentences unless they form one clear
comparison.

After a major figure, explain its meaning before introducing another figure.

Alternate between:

- Concrete situation.
- Explanation.
- Number.
- Interpretation.
- Consequence.
- Transition.

Do not use more than three consecutive sentences that only report facts.

Do not use more than two consecutive paragraphs without a concrete consequence,
decision, conflict, or example.

Every 45 to 90 seconds of narration should contain at least one of:

- A new discovery.
- A changed assumption.
- A concrete consequence.
- A meaningful comparison.
- A decision point.
- A counterargument.
- A shift in scale from company to sector, or sector to household.

---

## 10. Research Notes Are Not Narration

Research briefs may contain source disputes, alternative figures, caveats,
methodological notes, long explanations, unresolved claims, and questions for
further research.

The final script must convert those notes into clean narration.

Do not read research notes aloud. Do not place these phrases in final narration:

- "Primary document not directly accessed."
- "Date of claim unconfirmed."
- "Figure attributed to media reports."
- "Source may reflect an older period."
- "Treat as a working estimate until confirmed."

Use `[VERIFY]` in the script where necessary, and place the detailed explanation
in the source audit file.

## 11. Production Workflow

```
Topic
→ angle options
→ approved angle
→ story design
→ research plan
→ research
→ claim ledger
→ source audit
→ structure proposal
→ structure approval
→ first narration draft
→ fact-to-story pass
→ prose pass
→ source and freshness audit
→ storyboard
```

A documentary script is not a collection of facts. It is a controlled sequence of
discoveries. Each fact must answer a question, complicate an assumption, reveal a
mechanism, show a consequence, or prepare the next question. If the viewer can
remove a paragraph without losing the logic of the investigation, the paragraph
does not belong in the script.
