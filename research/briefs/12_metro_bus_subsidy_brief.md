## Approved angle

**The Metro Bus Subsidy Machine** — Pakistan's flagship Metro Bus/BRT systems deliver
a visible political win at opening, but the fare is set below the true cost of
operation from day one, turning a celebrated infrastructure launch into a
compounding, largely invisible fiscal liability.

## Central question

Do Pakistan's flagship Metro Bus/BRT systems (Lahore, Islamabad, Peshawar, Multan,
Karachi) solve urban mobility, or are they subsidy commitments governments
underprice at launch and cannot sustain?

## Tentative thesis

The same underpricing-becomes-permanent-liability mechanism already documented in
this channel's PSM and PIA episodes applies to urban transit: each system's fare
was set for political acceptability rather than cost recovery, and the resulting
subsidy gap has grown rather than shrunk as more systems were added.

---

## Claims required (from the angle's sub-questions)

1. What each system actually costs to build vs. what was budgeted
2. What each system charges vs. what it costs to run, per passenger
3. What each provincial government currently pays in subsidy, and how that has
   changed over time
4. Whether ridership met the targets used to justify the investment
5. Whether Pakistan's farebox recovery is genuinely abnormal by world standards,
   or in line with typical subsidized-transit practice — the thesis must survive
   this test honestly, not assume the answer

### International farebox recovery context (for the counterargument)

- US national average farebox recovery: 16% (2024). Top 50 US transit agencies by
  operating expense averaged 36% (2019). Typical North American range: 20–30%.
  European median: ~44%. Canada: ~56%. Asia median: ~137% (skewed upward by a
  small number of self-supporting systems — Hong Kong MTR, Singapore — that use
  land-value-capture financing models not comparable to Pakistan's systems).
  [REPORTED — via WebSearch synthesis, multiple transit-industry sources
  including TransitWiki and Eno Center for Transportation]
- **This matters directly for the thesis's honesty**: Pakistan's systems recover
  roughly 24–35% of operating cost via fares, which is not dramatically below
  the North American norm and is in the same broad range as many subsidized
  Western transit systems. The sharper, more defensible version of the thesis is
  not "these systems are subsidized" (normal) but: (a) construction costs
  escalated significantly after initial budgets were approved, (b) ridership
  targets were missed by a wide margin at least once (Islamabad: 53% of target
  two years in), and (c) the subsidy commitment appears to have grown over time
  as more systems were added, without a clear public accounting of the combined,
  compounding total until recent reporting surfaced the Rs 21bn aggregate figure.

## Counterarguments and how they are addressed

1. **"All transit is subsidized worldwide — this is normal, not a scandal."** True,
   and the farebox-recovery benchmark data above shows Pakistan's systems are not
   wildly outside international norms on this specific metric. The script must
   engage this directly rather than imply subsidy alone is the failure — the
   sharper claims (cost escalation, missed ridership targets, and subsidy growth
   over time as more systems launched) carry the thesis instead.
2. **"These are essential infrastructure regardless of the balance sheet."**
   Legitimate — the episode should not argue these systems shouldn't exist, only
   that their fiscal commitment appears to have been underdisclosed or
   under-planned at launch relative to what it actually costs to run them.
3. **"Cost overruns happen on large infrastructure projects everywhere."** Also
   true globally. Peshawar's specific, well-documented escalation trajectory
   (PC-1 to final cost, Supreme Court involvement) is presented as a factual
   case rather than framed as uniquely Pakistani incompetence.

## Stakeholder map

- **Owners/operators:** Punjab Masstransit Authority (Lahore, Multan, Rawalpindi/Islamabad Punjab-side); Capital Development Authority (Islamabad); TransPeshawar / Urban Mobility Company (Peshawar); Sindh Mass Transit Authority / Green Line administration (Karachi, federally-initiated)
- **Regulators/funders:** Provincial finance departments; federal government (PSDP funding for Green Line specifically); Asian Development Bank (financed Peshawar BRT)
- **Capital providers:** Provincial development budgets; ADB; federal PSDP
- **Customers:** Daily commuters — approximate current combined ridership across all systems in the hundreds of thousands per day (individual system figures above)
- **Cost-bearers who don't ride:** General provincial/federal taxpayers, including in cities and regions with no BRT/Metro coverage at all, who fund the subsidy through general revenue
