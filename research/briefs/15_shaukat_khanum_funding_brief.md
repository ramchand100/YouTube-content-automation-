## Approved angle

**The Hospital Carrying the Gap** — Pakistan's public health system never
built cancer-treatment capacity near the scale of the country's cancer
burden, so a private, donor-funded hospital has absorbed a share of that gap
far larger than any one hospital should have to carry, funded by a
cross-subsidy model (paying patients, zakat, and diaspora donations) that
works today but depends on constant fundraising growth to keep pace with
rising costs.

## Central question

How does Pakistan's largest free-cancer-care institution actually pay for
itself, and what does that funding model reveal about the state's role in
public health?

## Tentative thesis

The hospital's free-treatment model is sustained by a layered cross-subsidy
(paying patients, domestic zakat, and diaspora donations), which has scaled
successfully so far but faces two structural pressures: it is compensating
for a public-sector capacity gap the state has never closed, and its costs
are partly dollar-denominated while most of its donor base is not.

---

## Claims required (from the angle's sub-questions)

1. What is the scale of SKMCH&RC's free-treatment commitment, and how does it compare to Pakistan's overall cancer burden?
2. How is the free-treatment model actually funded — what's the split between paying patients, domestic zakat/donations, and the endowment?
3. How large and how fast-growing is the diaspora/overseas fundraising channel specifically?
4. How large is Pakistan's public-sector cancer-care capacity, and how does it compare to what SKMCH&RC alone provides?
5. What cost and currency pressures does the model face as it scales, and is there evidence of a reserve/endowment strategy to manage that?

## Counterarguments and how they are addressed

**The angle's own flagged risk:** this could read as an implicit criticism
of a well-regarded charity rather than a systemic observation about state
capacity.

**How the research addresses it:** every figure above about SKMCH&RC's own
scale and growth (75%+ free treatment, Rs 88bn in free care since 1994, a
budget that grew ~24% in two years, a diaspora fundraising channel growing
~45% in two years) is genuinely impressive and should be presented as such
on its own terms. The "gap" argument in Part 4 rests entirely on Pakistan's
public-sector capacity data (27 centers, fewer than 250 oncologists
nationally), which is independent of anything about SKMCH&RC specifically —
the script can and should state plainly that the hospital's scale is not
the problem; the absence of comparable capacity elsewhere in the system is.
This keeps the "gap" framing an institutional/fiscal observation, not a
critique of the hospital.
