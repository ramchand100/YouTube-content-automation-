## SECTION 1 — The Unsolved Reality  (0:00 - 2:00)
*~280 words. On-the-ground entry point.*
*Rhythm: short punchy sentences. Echo a key word for weight. Name the anomaly before explaining it.*
*Transition: "That is not a malfunction." End by stating the episode's single question.*

In 2015, Pakistan Steel Mills stopped producing steel. The furnaces went cold. The orders stopped. The gates closed.

The payroll did not stop.

Today, roughly 9,000 employees remain on Pakistan Steel Mills' books [VERIFY]. They receive salaries. They carry pension entitlements. Some have been drawing those salaries for a decade without producing a single commercial ton of steel. The mill sits on approximately 19,000 acres of prime coastal land on the edge of Karachi. It generates no revenue. It produces no product. It costs the state billions of rupees every year just to stand still.

That is not a malfunction. That is a system working exactly as it was designed.

Pakistan Steel Mills is not an accident. It is the most visible monument to a decision made in January 1972, when Zulfikar Ali Bhutto's government nationalized 31 major industrial groups in a single executive order. Cotton mills, cement plants, chemical factories, engineering firms — transferred to state ownership overnight. Two years later, the banks followed. Then cotton ginning, rice husking, and flour milling.

The owners received compensation. Their managers were replaced by civil servants and political appointees. The factories continued to operate.

But the logic that had built those factories — ownership risk, profit motive, managerial accountability — was removed from the equation. And that logic does not return simply because you decide you want it back.

Pakistan carries more than 200 state-owned enterprises today [VERIFY]. Their combined losses run to hundreds of billions of rupees every year [VERIFY]. Every rupee of that loss comes from the national budget. The national budget is already short.

How Pakistan built this structure, what it destroyed in the process, and whether it can finally dismantle it is what this episode is about.

## SECTION 2 — The Paper Trail  (2:00 - 5:45)
*~530 words. PKR unit economics and financial anatomy.*

The arithmetic of nationalization's legacy lives in three numbers: what the state pays to keep failed enterprises alive, what the private sector does not build because of the uncertainty that nationalization created, and what the economy loses from both.

Start with Pakistan Steel Mills.

The mill was built between 1973 and 1985 with Soviet technical assistance and Soviet financing [SOURCE: PSM official history]. Its designed capacity was 1.1 million tons of steel per year [VERIFY]. It never consistently reached that output. At its operational peak in the 1990s, it produced somewhere between 500,000 and 800,000 tons annually [VERIFY]. By 2008, losses had accumulated to the point where the government attempted a formal privatization. By 2015, the mills were effectively shut down commercially [VERIFY].

Since closure, the government has continued paying salary costs, pension obligations, and basic maintenance for a facility producing no commercial output [VERIFY]. The accumulated state outlay since closure is estimated in the hundreds of billions of rupees [VERIFY] [SOURCE: Finance Division appropriations].

PIA tells a comparable story at a larger scale. Pakistan International Airlines was one of Asia's leading carriers in the 1960s, before full state control brought it under government management in the nationalization era. Its accumulated losses by the time of privatization in late 2024 stood at approximately Rs. 700 billion [VERIFY] [SOURCE: PIA annual accounts]. That number accumulated over decades of government-appointed management, bloated staffing, routes chosen on political rather than commercial grounds, and a workforce shielded from restructuring by political protection.

The electricity distribution companies — the DISCOs — operate on the same model. Seventeen state-owned distribution companies receive power from generation companies and deliver it to homes and factories. They lose between 15 and 25 percent of what they receive to technical failures and outright theft [VERIFY] [SOURCE: NEPRA State of Industry Report]. They bill consumers at regulated tariffs that do not cover their full costs. The shortfall between cost and revenue is one of the main foundations of Pakistan's Rs. 2.5 trillion power sector circular debt [VERIFY].

The gas distribution companies, SNGPL and SSGCL, operate identically — as we examined in the previous episode. Both are state-owned. Both are structurally loss-making. Both feed into circular debt.

Across the full SOE portfolio, the International Monetary Fund has estimated that enterprise losses cost Pakistan roughly 1.5 to 2 percent of GDP in the worst years [VERIFY] [SOURCE: IMF Article IV Pakistan]. At Pakistan's current economic size, that is approximately 1.5 to 2 trillion rupees per year [VERIFY]. That figure does not include the opportunity cost: what the economy would generate if those resources were deployed by private owners who stood to gain or lose personally from the outcome.

The subsidy mechanism is direct. SOE losses are covered by government transfers, which appear in the federal budget as grants to public sector enterprises. Those grants are funded by borrowing when revenues fall short, which in Pakistan is most years. The borrowing adds to the debt pile. The debt pile requires interest payments. Interest payments now absorb roughly half of Pakistan's total federal revenues [VERIFY] [SOURCE: Ministry of Finance Debt Policy Statement]. The SOE loss is not the cause of Pakistan's fiscal crisis. But it is a structural tributary that feeds the river every single year.

## SECTION 3 — The Field Reality  (5:45 - 9:30)
*~530 words. Ground-level mechanics.*

To understand why nationalization did lasting damage beyond the balance sheets, you need to understand what private capital does when it no longer trusts the state.

In January 1972, the Economic Reforms Order listed 31 categories of industry for immediate nationalization. The list included cotton and textile mills, cement factories, vegetable ghee plants, chemical manufacturers, engineering goods producers, steel and metal works, and shipping companies. In most cases, these were family-owned businesses — the industrial families of Punjab and urban Sindh who had built Pakistan's first wave of manufacturing in the 1950s and 1960s.

The state paid compensation. In many cases, that compensation was below what the assets were worth in a functioning market. The original owners had no meaningful recourse.

The lesson that capital absorbed was precise: in Pakistan, a business large enough to be visible is a business at risk of seizure. The rational response was not to stop being wealthy. It was to hold wealth in assets the state could not easily nationalize: land, gold, foreign currency held abroad, and trading businesses rather than manufacturing plants.

A trading company buys goods and sells them. It has no fixed plant. Its capital is mobile — inventory, receivables, relationships. A textile mill has machinery bolted to a concrete floor. It is fixed, visible, and vulnerable. After 1972, Pakistani capital began — slowly, then structurally — to prefer the former over the latter. This is one reason why Pakistan's economy is disproportionately trading-heavy and manufacturing-light compared to regional peers that did not nationalize. Bangladesh, South Korea, Taiwan — none of them put their industrialists through a confiscation event.

The industrialists who had the means moved capital and sometimes families entirely out of the country. Business families from Lahore and Karachi relocated to the Gulf, to the United Kingdom, to North America. The Pakistani diaspora that followed was partly cultural and partly a direct consequence of the signals the state had sent about the security of domestic investment.

Inside the nationalized factories, a different problem took hold. Government-appointed managers had no ownership stake in the outcome. Their job security came from political connections, not from factory performance. Workers' unions became powerful because managers lacked both the authority and the commercial incentive to enforce discipline. Overstaffing became structural: hiring was politically useful, firing carried political cost, and no market pressure existed to correct either.

The Supreme Court's 2006 intervention demonstrates how deeply this pattern is embedded. When the Musharraf government attempted to privatize Pakistan Steel Mills, a Supreme Court bench blocked the transaction, citing procedural irregularities and concerns about asset undervaluation. The mill remained in state hands. By the next serious attempt at privatization, it had stopped producing.

The PIA privatization that completed in late 2024 broke this pattern for the first time in a generation. A major state enterprise was actually sold to a private buyer [SOURCE: Dawn, Business Recorder, 2024]. Whether it holds — whether the new management genuinely restructures operations, renegotiates workforce terms, and runs the airline on commercial logic rather than political logic — will determine whether Pakistan has changed a system or merely completed a transaction.

## SECTION 4 — The Systemic Domino Effect  (9:30 - 12:30)
*~420 words. Macroeconomic impact.*

The consequences of a fifty-year SOE legacy compound across every layer of the economy, because they reinforce each other at every connection point.

Start with fiscal space. Every rupee the government transfers to a loss-making enterprise is a rupee that cannot go to primary healthcare, to a rural school, or to road maintenance. Pakistan's public health spending and education spending as a share of GDP are among the lowest in South Asia [VERIFY] [SOURCE: World Bank development indicators]. SOE losses are not the sole cause of that shortfall, but they are a structural drain on every other budget claim, every year.

The debt spiral follows directly. Government transfers to SOEs are funded by borrowing when revenues fall short, which is chronic in Pakistan's fiscal position. That borrowing increases the debt stock. The debt stock generates interest obligations. Interest payments now consume roughly half of federal revenues [VERIFY]. The SOE loss is a small tributary feeding a debt river that has become the central fiscal constraint on everything else the government tries to do.

The IMF enters at this point. Every program Pakistan has entered since 2019 has included explicit SOE conditions: privatize PIA, restructure the DISCOs, reduce transfers to loss-making enterprises. Pakistan agrees to these conditions to unlock financing. It then moves slowly, because the political economy of SOE reform is genuinely difficult. Nine thousand PSM employees represent votes — and their families, and their union leaders, and the politicians who represent their constituencies. Every government that has tried to move faster has faced a coalition of workers, opposition parties, and courts.

The manufacturing export consequence is less visible but carries long-term weight. Bangladesh, which did not go through a comparable nationalization wave, has built a textile export industry roughly six times the size of Pakistan's [VERIFY] [SOURCE: WTO trade data]. The capital that might have built Pakistan's export manufacturing went into real estate, gold, and trading instead. That is not a recoverable decision in one policy cycle. It is a structural output gap that compounds over decades.

The energy cost loop closes the circle. State-owned electricity distribution companies lose revenue through technical losses and theft. Those losses are socialized into higher tariffs for paying consumers, who include manufacturers. Higher industrial electricity costs reduce the competitiveness of Pakistani exports. The decision made in 1972 is still embedded in the electricity bill that a Faisalabad factory pays today.

## SECTION 5 — The Verdict & Future Outlook  (12:30 - End)
*~340 words. Realistic forecast and concrete implications.*

Pakistan has a genuine, if narrow, window.

The PIA privatization in late 2024 demonstrated that it is politically possible to transfer a major state enterprise to private ownership, even one with a resistant workforce and decades of accumulated losses. That matters not because PIA is the largest problem in the SOE portfolio — it is not — but because it broke the psychological precedent that major Pakistani privatizations always collapse before completion.

The DISCOs are next on the IMF's list. These are the electricity distribution companies that serve millions of consumers in every province. Privatizing them is harder than privatizing an airline, because each DISCO is a regional monopoly. Transferring a monopoly to a private owner without strong regulatory reform simply replaces a state monopoly with a private one. The private owner will extract profit from a captive consumer base without competitive pressure to improve service or reduce losses.

Genuine reform here requires two things that Pakistan has historically been reluctant to do simultaneously: privatize the distribution function and build a regulatory framework strong enough to protect consumers from the private owner who replaces the state. Both have to happen together. One without the other is a different problem, not a solution.

Pakistan Steel Mills represents a separate category entirely. It is a genuinely unviable industrial asset sitting on land that is worth more than the mill. The 19,000 acres it occupies, properly developed, would generate more economic activity than the mill produced at its peak. Unlocking that value requires resolving the workforce question — pensioners, long-serving employees, contractual obligations — in a way that is politically manageable rather than politically catastrophic.

The fifty-year lesson is not complicated. Ownership matters. Accountability follows ownership. When nobody is personally accountable for an outcome, the outcome tends to look like Pakistan Steel Mills in 2024: a payroll attached to no product, on land attached to no plan.

Whether this decade is the one where Pakistan finally builds its private industrial base on the other side of that lesson is still unresolved. The PIA sale was a start. One sale does not make a pattern.
