## Part 1 — The Port Built for Cargo That Never Came

In December 2024, an official told Pakistan's National Assembly that Gwadar port was fully operational and ready to handle any kind of cargo. [SOURCE: Arab News, 2024] That same year, the port moved a total of 34,000 tonnes of cargo, down from 592,000 tonnes the year before. [REPORTED: multiple outlets citing government figures; VERIFY exact publication dates before recording] Since the port opened, it has handled a cumulative total of 4,789 containers. Its own installed capacity is 240,000 containers a year. [REPORTED: government statement to the National Assembly, cited by press]

That is not a small gap between what was said and what the numbers show. It is worth holding onto, because it is the clearest single fact in this story.

Now put it next to the two ports on the other side of Karachi. In the same year, Karachi Port handled 53.95 million tonnes of cargo, the highest amount in its 138 year history. [SOURCE: multiple outlets citing Karachi Port Trust, FY2024-25] Port Qasim, its neighbour, handled 45.3 million tonnes. [SOURCE: Port Qasim Authority, official port performance data, accessed 2026-08-22] Between the two of them, they move somewhere close to 90 percent of everything Pakistan ships in or out by sea. [REPORTED: multiple sources]

Ninety nine million tonnes at Karachi and Port Qasim. Thirty four thousand tonnes at Gwadar. That is not a port that is simply smaller than its neighbours. That is a port that, by the numbers, has barely started.

Gwadar was never meant to be a footnote. It was designed and promoted as Pakistan's third option, a deep sea port built specifically so the country did not have to route almost all of its trade through one stretch of coastline near Karachi. Billions of dollars went into building it. This episode asks a simple question about that investment: why did the cargo never follow it?

## Part 2 — What Was Promised

Start with why Gwadar was chosen at all. It sits on the Arabian Sea, roughly 400 kilometres outside the Strait of Hormuz, the narrow passage through which close to a fifth of the world's oil and gas travels. [REPORTED] For ships moving goods between China and the Middle East, Africa, or Central Asia, a route through Gwadar can be shorter than the traditional path through the Strait of Malacca, which in theory means real savings in time and fuel. [REPORTED] On paper, the location made sense.

Gwadar's story as a modern port begins in February 2013, when Pakistan handed the second phase of its development to a Chinese state-owned company, China Overseas Port Holding Company. [REPORTED: multiple sources] That handover sat inside a much larger commitment. Investment pledges under the China Pakistan Economic Corridor started at around 46 billion dollars and eventually grew to around 62 billion dollars, a figure that at the time amounted to roughly a fifth of Pakistan's entire economy. [REPORTED: Middle East Institute analysis]

The port company itself put in around 1.62 billion dollars to build Gwadar's deep water facilities. [REPORTED: VERIFY exact figure and whether a separate later phase added further investment before recording] By 2016, the port had three berths and could formally handle 137,000 containers a year, a decade after the first phase of construction had finished. [REPORTED: multiple sources]

Around the port, the government set up the Gwadar Free Zone, an area where new businesses can set up with reduced taxes and fewer restrictions on moving goods in and out. The logic behind a free zone is straightforward: a factory that imports raw material, turns it into a finished product, and exports that product again should not have to pay full duty twice, once coming in and once going out. Waiving that cost is meant to attract manufacturers who would otherwise build their factory somewhere else. A company can join Gwadar's zone for as little as 500,000 dollars plus a registration fee. [REPORTED] Several dozen enterprises have registered there over the years, though only a handful are reported as fully operational. [REPORTED, VERIFY the exact current count before recording]

This was the case that was made for Gwadar: a deep water port, built with serious capital, backed by a free trade zone, connected to the rest of the country by new roads and eventually a railway, meant to give Pakistan a second major gateway to the sea and Balochistan a share of the growth that gateway would bring.

That was the plan. What actually happened in Gwadar's early years of operation looked nothing like it.

## Part 3 — Why the Cargo Didn't Follow

In 2017, a year after Gwadar had formally opened, the port received 145 containers of project cargo, materials for its own construction, and not a single general commercial cargo ship. [REPORTED] That is close to no trade at all for a facility built to handle over a hundred thousand containers a year.

Part of the answer is that the port was never finished in the way the plan required. A ship can dock at Gwadar, but the road and rail links that would let cargo actually move in and out of the country efficiently were not built at the same pace as the berths.

The main road connecting Gwadar to the rest of Pakistan, the M8 motorway, was planned to run nearly 900 kilometres to Ratodero in Sindh. A 193 kilometre stretch from Gwadar opened in 2016. Other sections remain incomplete even now, despite planning that goes back close to two decades. [REPORTED] A direct rail link connecting Gwadar to the national railway network has not been built at all. It is currently listed as a mid term project, expected to start in 2025 and finish by 2030. [REPORTED] Which means that as of today, a port promoted as Pakistan's future gateway still has no working rail connection to the rest of the country.

A report prepared for the Prime Minister on Gwadar's operations pointed to several other reasons cargo has stayed away: high costs of running the port, a lack of supporting infrastructure, and limited interest from international shipping companies. [REPORTED: report to the Prime Minister, cited by press] It also pointed to security concerns serious enough that global insurance firms have been reluctant to cover cargo passing through the port. That detail matters more than it might sound like. Before a shipping company sends a vessel anywhere, it needs insurance for the ship and its cargo. If insurers see a route as risky, they charge more for that cover, or decline to offer it at all. Either way, the shipping company's cost of using that route goes up, and a route that costs more gets chosen less. A handful of insurance decisions made far from Gwadar can therefore do as much to keep cargo away as any missing road. [REPORTED]

None of this means the original decision to build a port at Gwadar was wrong. Deep water access on that stretch of coast is real, and the location sits close to shipping lanes that matter for the wider region. What the evidence shows is narrower and more specific: the connections a port needs to actually move cargo, roads that work end to end, a railway, lower costs, and enough confidence from shipping companies to send their ships there, were promised well before they were delivered. A berth without a finished road to it is a berth ships have very little reason to use.

## Part 4 — What Concentration Costs, and Whether Gwadar Can Still Matter

Nearly all of Pakistan's seaborne trade currently depends on two ports sitting close together on one stretch of Karachi's coastline. That is not, by itself, a crisis. It is a concentration, and any concentration carries a cost: if something disrupts operations at either port, weather, a labour dispute, congestion, there is no working alternative absorbing that trade. Almost every container of imported machinery, every tonne of imported fuel, every export shipment of textiles or rice leaving the country passes through the same short stretch of coast. A third working port nearby would not remove that risk completely, but it would give the country somewhere for at least some of that cargo to go if the other two are disrupted at the same time. Gwadar was supposed to be that alternative. For most of its history, it has not been able to play that role.

To be fair to the more recent numbers, 2026 has looked different. In April alone, Gwadar processed around 11,000 containers, more cargo than it handled across the whole of 2025. Over a three month period, the port moved roughly 200,000 tonnes of cargo passing through on its way to other destinations. [REPORTED] That is real growth, and it deserves to be stated plainly rather than dismissed.

It also deserves a closer look. Reporting on that growth ties much of it to disruption in shipping through the Strait of Hormuz, the narrow passage most Gulf shipping normally relies on. [REPORTED] When that route becomes less reliable, some cargo gets redirected to ports nearby, Gwadar among them. That is a real increase in traffic. It is not the same thing as Pakistan finally completing the roads, the railway, and the cost reductions the original plan depended on. If Hormuz shipping returns to normal, there is no evidence yet that this year's cargo stays.

The honest answer to why Gwadar hasn't become what it was promised to be is not a single failure or a single decision. It is that the parts of the plan that determine whether a port actually gets used, finished roads, a working railway, manageable costs, and enough confidence from shipping companies to route cargo there routinely, were treated as things that could follow the port's opening rather than requirements that had to be in place before it. Karachi Port and Port Qasim did not out compete Gwadar because they are better ports. They out competed it because they were already connected, already trusted, and already carrying the trade Gwadar was meant to share.

Whether that changes is now a question about roads still being built and a railway still years from completion, not about the port itself. What would actually need to happen is specific, not vague: the remaining stretches of the M8 motorway would need to open, the rail link would need to move from a mid term plan to a finished line, and shipping companies and insurers would need enough confidence in the route to choose it on their own, without a Hormuz disruption forcing their hand. None of that is impossible. None of it has happened yet either. Until those pieces exist, Pakistan's seaborne trade will keep moving through the same stretch of Karachi coastline it always has, and the port built to change that will keep waiting for the cargo it was designed to carry.
