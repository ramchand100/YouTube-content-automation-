## SECTION 1 — The Unsolved Reality  (0:00 - 2:00)
*~310 words.*

[VISUAL 00:00 — early morning in a working-class neighbourhood. A daily-wage labourer walks his young daughter past a large government school with a locked gate, and into a small private school run out of a rented house down the street.]

Every morning, a labourer who earns maybe a thousand rupees a day walks his daughter to school. On the way, they pass a government school. It is free. Their taxes already paid for it. He does not send her there. Instead, he walks another five minutes to a small private school, squeezed into a rented house, and he pays a fee he can barely afford.

[VISUAL 00:35 — text builds on black: "The free school is right there. Millions of poor families walk past it."]

Multiply that one father by millions, and you have one of the strangest facts about Pakistan. This is a poor country. Money is tight for almost everyone. And yet families near the bottom of the income ladder are choosing to pay for school, when a free one is sitting right there.

[VISUAL 01:05 — two numbers rise and hold: "PRIVATE SCHOOLS: ~44% of students" and "OUT OF SCHOOL: ~26 million kids".]

Today, private schools teach close to half of all school-going children in Pakistan [SOURCE: ADB / RISE, 2017-18] [VERIFY]. Most of these are not fancy elite academies. They are cheap, no-frills schools in ordinary streets. At the same time, around twenty-six million children are not in any school at all, one of the largest out-of-school populations on earth [SOURCE: Save the Children / UNICEF, 2024-2025] [VERIFY].

[VISUAL 01:40 — title card on black: "PAYING FOR FREE", green accent on "FREE".]

So we have two puzzles sitting on top of each other. Why do the poor pay for something the state gives away for free? And why, even so, are tens of millions of children still left out completely? In this brief, we follow the money into a low-fee private school, we go inside the free school to see what actually happens there, and we work out what this quietly says about Pakistan's future.

## SECTION 2 — The Paper Trail  (2:00 - 6:00)
*~590 words.*

[VISUAL 02:00 — a simple animated timeline of private-school growth begins to draw.]

Let us follow the money, because the numbers tell a clear story.

Start with how fast the private system grew. In 1982, Pakistan had around three thousand private schools. By 2015, it had more than seventy thousand [SOURCE: ADB brief; education studies] [VERIFY]. In the province of Punjab alone, the number of low-cost private schools roughly doubled in about twenty-five years [SOURCE: education studies]. This was not planned by any government. It was millions of families voting with their own money.

[VISUAL 02:45 — the free school side of the screen shrinks as a budget bar drops: "GOV EDUCATION SPEND ~0.8% of GDP".]

Now look at the free system they are walking away from. The government spends only about zero point eight percent of the country's total income on education. That is a record low, down from around two percent a few years ago [SOURCE: Save the Children; Pakistan Economic Survey 2024-25] [VERIFY]. Global advice is to spend four to six percent [SOURCE: Incheon Declaration]. In one recent year, education spending actually fell by nearly a third [SOURCE: Save the Children, 2025] [VERIFY]. So the free school is not just free. It is starved. And starved schools show it.

[VISUAL 03:40 — zoom into a single low-fee private school; a simple ledger appears.]

Next, the economics of the cheap private school itself, because this is the clever part. A low-fee private school charges a small monthly fee, often only a few hundred to a couple of thousand rupees per child [SOURCE: low-fee private schooling studies] [VERIFY]. How can it survive on so little? By keeping costs brutally low. It rents a house instead of building a campus. It hires young, often untrained teachers, usually local women, on very low salaries [SOURCE: low-fee private schooling research] [VERIFY]. It packs in enough students that many small fees add up. It is a volume business built on low pay.

[VISUAL 04:30 — a family budget: "Income Rs30,000/month" with fees for three children peeling off a visible chunk.]

For the family, though, the fee is not small. Picture a household earning thirty thousand rupees a month with three school-age children. Even at a few hundred rupees each, plus uniforms, books, and transport, school becomes one of the biggest fixed costs in the home. And they pay it anyway, month after month. That tells you how little they trust the free option.

[VISUAL 05:15 — define-on-screen card: "SHADOW EDUCATION = extra paid tuition on top of regular school."]

Now add one more layer that surprises people. Even children who are in school, public or private, often pay again in the evening for private tuition. Teachers call it "shadow education", meaning extra paid coaching on top of normal school. By some surveys, roughly two in three urban children attend evening tuition, with fees running anywhere from a thousand rupees a month to tens of thousands for exam classes [SOURCE: tuition-demand studies; ADB/CERC]. So many families are effectively paying for school twice. Once for the classroom, and again for the coaching that the classroom did not deliver. The paper trail is clear. Pakistanis are spending more and more of their own money to educate their children, precisely because the free system is not doing the job.

## SECTION 3 — The Field Reality  (6:00 - 10:00)
*~590 words.*

[VISUAL 06:00 — inside a neglected government school: an empty classroom, a broken fan, no running water, a register lying open on a dusty desk.]

To understand why families walk past a free school, you have to step inside one.

[VISUAL 06:30 — a graphic term appears: "GHOST SCHOOL" and "GHOST TEACHER", with plain definitions.]

Two phrases explain a lot. The first is "ghost school", a school that exists on the government's paper records but barely functions in real life, or does not open at all. The second is "ghost teacher", a teacher who is on the payroll, drawing a salary every month, but who rarely or never shows up to teach [SOURCE: reporting on ghost schools and teachers, 2023-2024]. By official estimates, Pakistan has around twenty-five thousand ghost teachers, and well over a thousand ghost schools in Punjab alone, with more in other provinces [SOURCE: reporting, 2023-2024] [VERIFY]. That is public money paying for teaching that never happens.

[VISUAL 07:20 — a real-feeling classroom scene: a handful of students, no teacher, kids waiting.]

Now add the schools that do open but barely run. Many government schools lack basic things: fans in brutal heat, clean water, working toilets [SOURCE: reporting on school conditions]. Teachers, even the real ones, are often absent, pulled into other government duties or simply not showing up. So a poor parent sees, with their own eyes, a free school where the teacher may not come and little gets taught. Against that, a small private school in a rented house has one thing going for it that matters enormously. The owner is a local businessperson whose income depends on parents staying happy, so if a teacher does not show up, that teacher is replaced. Presence, not prestige, is what the parent is buying.

[VISUAL 08:15 — a busy evening tuition academy: rows of students, a whiteboard, a fee receipt.]

Then there is the parallel world of tuition. Walk through any city in the evening and you will find coaching academies packed with students, long after school has closed. This is a large cash economy in its own right, with fees that can rival the school itself [SOURCE: tuition-demand studies]. It exists because the school day, public or private, often does not teach enough for a child to actually pass or compete. So families pay for school, then pay again for the tuition that makes the school work.

[VISUAL 08:40 — a chalkboard with children copying lines by rote; a stack of exam guidebooks and "guess papers" beside it.]

Much of what does get taught is built around passing exams, not understanding. Children memorize answers and copy notes, and a whole industry of guidebooks and "guess papers", cheap booklets that predict exam questions, grows up around it. A child can move up a grade by memorizing without ever really learning to think. So even a family that pays, shows up, and does everything right can end up with a certificate that means more on paper than in the child's head.

[VISUAL 09:00 — a plain bar chart: "Grade 5 children who can read a Grade 2 story: ~half".]

And here is the hard part. After all this spending, the results are still weak. In a major national survey, only about half of children could read a simple story meant for a child three years younger, and fewer than half could do basic grade-three arithmetic [SOURCE: ASER Pakistan, 2023] [VERIFY]. Private schools do tend to score a bit better than government ones, which is part of why parents choose them. But the honest picture is that the whole system, paid or free, is struggling to teach the basics. Families are paying more and more, and still not getting the one thing they are paying for: a child who can read, write, and count with confidence.

## SECTION 4 — The Systemic Domino Effect  (10:00 - 13:00)
*~450 words.*

[VISUAL 10:00 — pull back from one street to a national map dotted with school icons, many faded or dark.]

Now zoom out, because a classroom problem this big becomes an economy problem.

[VISUAL 10:35 — a large figure holds: "~26 million out of school", then a caption: "the future workforce".]

Start with the twenty-six million children out of school, plus the millions inside school who are not really learning. Put those together and you get a generation entering adult life without strong basic skills [SOURCE: out-of-school and learning data, 2023-2025]. In earlier videos we saw why Pakistan's exports stay stuck and its factories struggle to move up to higher-value work. This is a big part of the reason. You cannot build a modern, high-value economy on a workforce that cannot comfortably read a manual or do the math a skilled job needs. Education is the deepest root of the productivity problem.

[VISUAL 11:10 — two rows of children, one of boys and one of girls; part of the girls' row fades out.]

The gap is widest for girls. Among the children left out of school, more are girls than boys [SOURCE: Save the Children / UNICEF, 2024-2025] [VERIFY]. When a poor family can only afford to educate some of its children, or when the nearest safe, functioning school is too far, it is most often the daughter who is kept home. So the failure of the free system does not fall evenly. It falls hardest on the girls, and a country that educates fewer of its women pays for it later in almost every measure of development.

[VISUAL 11:25 — a widening gap graphic: one child climbing a staircase labelled "paid school + tuition", another stuck at the bottom by a "ghost school".]

Then there is fairness. The private boom quietly splits the next generation in two. Families with some money buy their way to a better school and evening tuition, and their children pull ahead. The poorest, who cannot pay, are left with the ghost schools and the broken fans. So the education system, instead of lifting people up equally, starts to lock inequality in place, passing advantage and disadvantage from one generation to the next.

[VISUAL 12:05 — a shrinking government-budget slice for education, next to a growing population of children.]

And the state is moving the wrong way. As the number of children grows, the share of national income spent on education has been falling, not rising [SOURCE: Save the Children; Economic Survey 2024-25]. The government, short of tax money and heavy with debt, keeps trimming the one budget that shapes the country's long-term future. It is the same false economy we keep seeing. Cheap today, ruinously expensive tomorrow, paid not in this year's budget but in decades of lost potential.

## SECTION 5 — The Verdict & Future Outlook  (13:00 - End)
*~360 words.*

[VISUAL 13:00 — clean slate, one line: "IS THE PRIVATE BOOM GOOD NEWS?"]

So what do we make of all this?

[VISUAL 13:20 — two readings of the same graph appear: "hope" and "warning".]

The private school boom can be read two ways, and both are true. The hopeful reading is that even the poorest Pakistani families deeply want education and will sacrifice real money for it. That hunger is one of the country's greatest assets. The warning reading is that the boom only exists because the state quietly walked away from a basic duty, and handed it, by default, to whoever could pay.

[VISUAL 13:55 — a simple split: "fix the free system" on one side, "regulate quality" on the other.]

The fix is not to attack private schools. They are a response to a real failure, not the cause of it. The real work is less exciting and more important. Make the free system actually function: end the ghost schools, make sure teachers show up, train and pay them properly, and spend meaningfully more than zero point eight percent of national income. And set a floor for quality, so that a cheap private school is held to a basic standard, not just left to the market.

[VISUAL 14:30 — return to the opening street: the labourer and his daughter at the private school gate.]

Here is the verdict. A country where poor parents pay to escape their own free schools is telling you something plainly. The demand for education is not the problem. The supply is. Until the free system earns back the trust those parents have already withdrawn, the pattern holds. Those who can pay, pay twice. Those who cannot, fall further behind.

[VISUAL 14:50 — three quiet takeaway lines build, then cut to black and the end card.]

For founders, the opening is huge: low-cost school networks, teacher training, affordable learning tools, and honest testing that tells parents what a child actually knows. For employers, the skills gap you complain about starts in a grade-two classroom. And for anyone reading Pakistan's future, watch one number above all: how much the country is willing to spend, and actually deliver, on teaching its own children.
