## SECTION 1 — The Unsolved Reality  (0:00 - 2:00)
*~284 words. On-the-ground entry point.*

In January 2022, Pakistan's government issued a tender to buy a spot cargo of LNG. It needed the gas to keep factories running and homes heated through the winter. The government set a price cap on what it was willing to pay. No supplier responded. Not one.

A month later, Russia invaded Ukraine. European countries scrambled to replace Russian pipeline gas. LNG tankers that might have sailed toward Karachi turned around and headed for Rotterdam and Bilbao instead. Spot LNG prices on the global market climbed to seventy dollars per unit, roughly seven times what Pakistan pays under its long-term contracts. At that price, buying enough to fill the shortfall would have cost more than Pakistan's entire energy subsidy budget.

The result: scheduled gas cuts of twelve to eighteen hours a day across industrial areas of Faisalabad, Lahore, and Sialkot. Textile mills, fertilizer plants, and small factories went dark for days at a time. CNG stations across Punjab closed indefinitely.

This is the LNG trap. It is not a crisis that came from nowhere. It is the end result of fifty years of decisions: gas prices held artificially low, domestic exploration starved of investment, and a growing dependence on a global commodity market that Pakistan is far too small to influence.

Pakistan discovered gas at Sui, Balochistan, in 1952. That discovery powered the country's first wave of industrialization. For five decades, Pakistan burned through its domestic reserves while keeping prices low enough that no one had a reason to find new ones. By the time the old fields started declining, there was no plan for what came next.

Today, the bill arrives at two terminals on the Karachi waterfront, priced in dollars that Pakistan does not have in abundance. That bill, and why it keeps growing, is what this episode is about.

## SECTION 2 — The Paper Trail  (2:00 - 5:45)
*~527 words. PKR unit economics and financial anatomy.*

To understand the trap, start with Pakistan's gas balance sheet.

Pakistan produces roughly 3.5 billion cubic feet of gas per day from its own fields [VERIFY] [SOURCE: OGRA Annual Report]. Its economy demands roughly six billion cubic feet per day [VERIFY] [SOURCE: PBS Energy Yearbook]. That is a daily gap of about 2.5 billion cubic feet. Every single day, that gap has to be filled from somewhere. LNG is how Pakistan fills it.

LNG stands for Liquefied Natural Gas. Natural gas cooled to minus 162 degrees Celsius shrinks to one six-hundredth of its original volume. At that size, it fits in a ship. When the tanker docks at Karachi, a re-gasification terminal heats the liquid back up and turns it into gas, then feeds it into the national pipeline network. The terminal is the bridge between the global LNG market and Pakistani homes, factories, and power plants.

Pakistan has two re-gasification terminals at Port Qasim, on the eastern edge of Karachi harbour: the Engro Elengy Terminal and Pakistan GasPort. Together they can process roughly 1,200 million cubic feet of gas per day [VERIFY] [SOURCE: EETPL/PGPCL terminal filings].

Pakistan buys LNG under two types of contracts.

The first is a long-term contract, typically fifteen to twenty years, where the price is linked to global crude oil prices. Pakistan's main long-term supplier is Qatar Energy. That contract covers roughly 400 million cubic feet per day at a price of around thirteen percent of Brent crude [VERIFY] [SOURCE: PLL / Ministry of Energy]. When Brent trades at eighty dollars a barrel, Pakistan pays roughly ten dollars per mmbtu, the standard unit of energy in gas contracts. Converted to rupees at current exchange rates, that is over Rs. 2,800 per mmbtu [VERIFY].

The second type is spot LNG: gas bought on the open market, cargo by cargo, at whatever price the global market charges that week. In normal conditions, spot prices track close to long-term contract prices. In a supply crunch, they are not close at all. In 2022, spot LNG hit seventy dollars per mmbtu, roughly seven times the long-term contract price [SOURCE: ICIS / JKM index].

Here is where the gas bill becomes a fiscal problem.

OGRA, Pakistan's gas regulator, sets the price at which consumers buy gas from the national grid. That regulated price is often below what importing the gas actually costs. The difference does not disappear. It sits on the books of Sui Northern Gas Pipelines, which serves Punjab and KPK, and Sui Southern Gas Company, which serves Sindh and Balochistan, as unpaid receivables.

Those companies cannot fully pay Pakistan LNG Limited, the state entity that procures the cargoes. PLL cannot fully pay the terminal operators. The terminal operators cannot service their financing in full. The same chain of unpaid bills that created the power sector's circular debt is running in parallel here, in gas. Pakistan's gas circular debt has grown to over Rs. 2 trillion [VERIFY] [SOURCE: OGRA / Finance Division]. It grows every quarter because the structural gap between what gas costs to import and what consumers pay for it has never been honestly closed.

Every ten rupees that the rupee falls against the dollar adds directly to this bill. LNG is bought in dollars. It is sold to Pakistanis in rupees at a regulated tariff that does not keep pace with the exchange rate. Devaluation is not an abstract macro problem for the gas sector. It arrives as a larger invoice every month.

## SECTION 3 — The Field Reality  (5:45 - 9:30)
*~527 words. Ground-level mechanics.*

The paper trail shows the math. The field reality shows why the math never closes.

Pakistan's gas system was designed in the 1960s and 1970s, when domestic production was growing and the country could afford to keep prices low as a matter of industrial policy. Cheap gas made fertilizer cheap. Cheap fertilizer made wheat cheap. Cheap wheat kept food prices low and politics stable. That logic made sense when the gas was there. It created a dependency that no government since has had the political will to undo.

The exploration and production sector, the companies that drill for gas, faced the same trap from the other side. Gas produced from domestic fields is sold to the distribution companies at OGRA-regulated prices. For most of the past two decades, those prices were set below the cost of finding and extracting new gas. When you cannot sell your product at a profit, you stop making it. Pakistan's exploration companies responded rationally: they reduced exploration spending. Why spend billions drilling for gas you cannot sell at a profit?

The result is visible in the numbers. Pakistan's domestic gas output peaked around 2012 and has declined by roughly five to eight percent a year since [VERIFY] [SOURCE: OGRA Annual Report]. The fields at Sui, Kandhkot, and Mari that powered the economy for fifty years are mature. They cannot produce more by pushing harder. New production requires new wells in new areas, and new wells require prices that make drilling worth the risk.

On the demand side, the system has its own distortion. Pakistan operates a dual-pricing structure: cheap domestic gas goes to priority sectors, including households, fertilizer plants, and certain power plants, while expensive LNG goes to industrial users lower on the priority list. Being allocated cheap domestic gas instead of expensive LNG is worth real money to a factory owner. It is the difference between a competitive cost structure and one that prices you out of your market. Allocation decisions, in Pakistan, attract the kind of attention that valuable decisions always attract.

There is also unaccounted-for gas: gas that enters the distribution network and does not come out metered and billed at the other end. This covers physical leaks in aging infrastructure, meter inaccuracies, and outright theft. Pakistan's distribution system loses an estimated ten to fifteen percent of throughput to unaccounted-for gas [VERIFY] [SOURCE: OGRA UFG reports]. In a system already losing money on every unit of LNG it imports, losing a further ten to fifteen percent for free makes the economics structurally impossible to fix at the tariff level alone.

The 2022 crisis exposed all of this at once. When no LNG cargo was available at a price Pakistan could afford, the entire shortfall fell on the pipeline grid. Distribution companies had to decide who got gas and who did not. Industrial users, who had no alternative and could not operate without gas, went dark. A textile mill in Faisalabad cannot switch to diesel the way a vehicle can switch to CNG. The shutdown was direct and immediate. [SOURCE: Dawn energy desk, Business Recorder, January-April 2022]

The lesson from 2022 was not that LNG is unreliable. It was that Pakistan had built an industrial economy on a fuel it does not control, at a price it had never honestly paid, through infrastructure it had never adequately maintained.

## SECTION 4 — The Systemic Domino Effect  (9:30 - 12:30)
*~424 words. Macroeconomic impact.*

When gas becomes expensive or disappears, the consequences do not stay in the energy sector.

Start with fertilizer. Pakistan's urea, the most widely used fertilizer for wheat and rice, is made by reacting natural gas with nitrogen drawn from the air. Gas is the raw material, not just the fuel. When gas prices rise or supply is cut, fertilizer plants either reduce output or pass the cost directly into the price of urea. A farmer planting wheat across five acres cannot absorb a thirty percent increase in fertilizer costs without cutting somewhere: crop area, input quality, or his own income. Gas price policy and national wheat production are connected through a chain that runs from the wellhead to the field to the flour mill to the kitchen table. [SOURCE: NFDC / fertilizer sector data]

The power sector carries a parallel transmission. A significant share of Pakistan's electricity comes from gas-fired plants. When LNG is expensive, the fuel cost component of electricity generation rises. That feeds into tariffs. The power sector and gas sector do not have separate circular debts; they feed each other. Unpaid bills in the gas sector reduce gas supply to power plants. Less gas means less generation. Less generation means more load shedding. More load shedding means less industrial output. Less industrial output means less income, less tax revenue, and a wider fiscal deficit.

SBP carries the foreign exchange impact. Pakistan's LNG import bill is settled in US dollars. In a normal year, that bill runs to three to four billion dollars [VERIFY] [SOURCE: SBP balance of payments]. Every billion dollars of LNG imports is a billion that does not go toward debt repayment, medicine, or industrial machinery. The gas bill competes with every other hard currency obligation Pakistan carries, at a time when those obligations are already tight.

FBR feels the industrial shutdown directly. Factories that close during gas shortages produce less, sell less, and pay less in sales tax, income tax, and withholding tax. The losses from weeks of factory shutdowns across Punjab in 2022 do not appear in a single headline, but they appear in the tax collection data for those months.

The trap reinforces itself. Because tariffs are held below import cost, distribution companies cannot invest in pipeline maintenance or loss reduction. Because exploration prices are held below drilling breakeven, new domestic gas is not found. Because domestic gas declines, LNG dependence grows. Because LNG is priced in dollars, every devaluation makes the import bill larger. The cycle has no automatic exit.

## SECTION 5 — The Verdict & Future Outlook  (12:30 - End)
*~338 words. Realistic forecast and concrete implications.*

Pakistan cannot exit the LNG market quickly. The dependence is structural and will take years to reduce even with the right decisions made today.

The most credible domestic option is developing Balochistan's underdeveloped gas fields and Pakistan's offshore blocks, which remain largely unexplored. Making that happen requires wellhead prices that make exploration commercially viable, a stable regulatory framework that gives companies confidence to commit capital to ten-year projects, and a government that does not suppress prices again the moment production increases and politics get uncomfortable.

The TAPI pipeline, Turkmenistan-Afghanistan-Pakistan-India, has been on the drawing board since the 1990s. It would bring Central Asian gas into the Pakistani grid at a lower cost than LNG. It also crosses 800 kilometres of Afghanistan, making it one of the most complex infrastructure proposals ever attempted. TAPI is not a solution for this decade.

The fastest pressure-relief valve for gas demand is the power sector. Pakistan's rapid solar expansion is already reducing the amount of gas-fired electricity the grid needs to generate. Every megawatt of rooftop solar is one fewer unit of LNG the system needs to buy. This reduction is happening now, driven by economics, not policy. It will continue.

The honest verdict: Pakistan will remain structurally dependent on LNG for at least the next decade. The question is not how to escape the market but how to manage the dependence less expensively. That means larger long-term contracts to reduce spot exposure, cost-reflective tariffs that stop the circular debt from growing, and serious capital deployed into domestic exploration before the remaining fields decline further.

The 2022 crisis was a preview. Global LNG markets will tighten again. How exposed Pakistan is when they do will be determined by decisions being made, or avoided, right now.
