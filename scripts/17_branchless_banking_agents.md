## Editorial frame

### Approved angle

The Agent Economy That Banked Pakistan's Unbanked — a network of small shopkeepers turned into cash-in/cash-out agents for Easypaisa and JazzCash gave tens of millions of Pakistanis their first mobile-wallet access without a bank branch, but the same unit economics that made the network spread fast in cities is why it still thins out where financial inclusion was supposed to matter most, and why a chunk of that economy may run on workarounds the companies never see.

### Central question

How does a branchless-banking agent actually make money on cash-in/cash-out transactions, how do agents' own alleged workarounds reveal a gap between the official commission structure and what actually happens at the counter, and does all of that explain both where the network reached and why Raast could now undercut the model that built it?

### Thesis

Commission structures set by the wallet operators made agent density a function of transaction volume and liquidity cost, not policy intent, explaining the network's urban-heavy geography. A separate layer of agent-side workarounds is described by industry sources but not independently corroborated for Pakistan — the script treats it as unresolved. Raast's free, instant rail is a structurally supported anticipated threat to the commission model this whole network runs on, not yet a measured decline.

### Story logic

Financial, with an institutional throughline. The episode follows how money actually moves at an agent's counter — what the operator pays, what the customer pays, what the regulator requires — and treats geographic reach and the Raast threat as consequences of that mechanism rather than separate stories.

### Structure rationale

Seven parts, approved by the user. Part 1 opens on the scale of the network against its own gender and inclusion gap. Parts 2 and 3 build the commission mechanism in two honestly separated layers: the official, regulated structure, then the harder, unresolved question of what agents may keep beyond it. Part 4 tests that economics against real geography. Part 5 introduces Raast as the structural threat to the entire mechanism just built. Part 6 reconciles the achievement against the gaps. Part 7 synthesizes without new figures.

## Part 1 — The Anomaly

By the third quarter of 2025, Pakistan's mobile wallets held roughly 141.6 million registered accounts, serviced by 757,727 branchless-banking agents nationwide. [SOURCE: State Bank of Pakistan, Branchless Banking Statistics, 2025] [VERIFY]

Only 14 percent of Pakistani women, though, have access to a formal financial account of any kind, against 56 percent of men. [SOURCE: Karandaaz Financial Inclusion Survey, 2024] [VERIFY]

A branchless-banking agent is a shopkeeper authorized by a mobile-wallet operator like Easypaisa or JazzCash to convert cash into a digital wallet balance, and back again, from a normal shop counter instead of a bank branch.

That 141.6 million account total is still climbing fast, up from 135.9 million just the previous quarter. [SOURCE: State Bank of Pakistan, Branchless Banking Statistics, 2025] [VERIFY] [ESTIMATE — roughly 5.7 million new accounts added in a single quarter]

Of the 757,727 registered agents counted that quarter, only 239,034 actually processed a transaction. [SOURCE: State Bank of Pakistan, Branchless Banking Statistics, 2025] [VERIFY] That's roughly one agent in three doing any business at all, the same quarter the network's headline numbers were being reported. [ESTIMATE — 239,034 divided by 757,727]

That scale is a real achievement, not a mirage. Financial inclusion, meaning the share of adults with access to a formal financial account, rose from 8 percent in 2013 to 35 percent in 2024. [SOURCE: Karandaaz Financial Inclusion Survey, 2024] [VERIFY]

Mobile money drove almost all of that rise. Usage went from under 1 percent of adults to 30 percent over the same decade. [SOURCE: Karandaaz Financial Inclusion Survey, 2024] [VERIFY]

But that 35 percent figure hides who actually got included. The gender gap in Part 1's opening line, 14 percent of women against 56 percent of men, is a four-fold difference the mobile-wallet era was supposed to shrink, not preserve. [SOURCE: Karandaaz Financial Inclusion Survey, 2024] [ANALYSIS] [VERIFY]

Part of that gap starts before a wallet is ever opened. Only 46 percent of women in Pakistan own a mobile phone at all, against 82 percent of men. [SOURCE: Karandaaz Financial Inclusion Survey, 2024] [VERIFY]

The same survey found that once a woman does own a mobile-money wallet, her comfort using it is actually higher than assumptions about digital literacy would predict. [SOURCE: Karandaaz Financial Inclusion Survey, 2024] [ANALYSIS] [VERIFY] The block sits earlier, at phone ownership and registration, not at willingness to use the technology once someone has it.

Geography complicates the picture further. Punjab's overall financial inclusion rate reached 40 percent in 2024, against 38 percent in Islamabad and 33 percent in Gilgit-Baltistan. [SOURCE: Karandaaz Financial Inclusion Survey, 2024] [VERIFY]

That Islamabad, the federal capital and one of the country's wealthier districts, sits behind Punjab is a sign that income alone doesn't explain this map. [ANALYSIS]

So a network large enough to hold accounts for well over half the adult population still leaves a specific, identifiable slice of the country behind. That gap is the puzzle this episode follows: how an agent actually gets paid on every cash-in and cash-out, what agents may do to keep more of that money than the official model allows, and whether either of those explains where this network reached, and where it didn't.

## Part 2 — How an Agent Is Supposed to Make Money

Easypaisa is run by Telenor Microfinance Bank, and JazzCash by Mobilink Microfinance Bank, the two licensed microfinance banks that dominate Pakistan's mobile-wallet market. Both set the commission terms every agent under their brand actually works under.

Two transactions sit at the center of every agent's counter. Cash-in is when a customer hands over rupees and the agent credits their wallet; cash-out is when a customer withdraws rupees against a wallet balance they already hold.

The general model researchers describe across mobile-money markets like Pakistan runs the same way on both sides. Cash-in is meant to cost the customer nothing, with the wallet operator instead paying the agent a small commission out of its own margin. [SOURCE: CGAP, "Wallet and OTC Transactions: Understanding Financial Incentives," 2017]

Cash-out is the side that carries a fee. The customer pays it directly, and the agent again earns a cut. [SOURCE: CGAP, "Wallet and OTC Transactions: Understanding Financial Incentives," 2017]

The clearest measured snapshot of what that commission is actually worth in Pakistan is CGAP fieldwork from 2013 to 2016 — nearly a decade old now, but the best available benchmark for the mechanics. [SOURCE: CGAP, 2017] [VERIFY — historical benchmark, not current pricing]

On a $20 reference transaction, CGAP found an agent's own commission on a wallet cash-in or cash-out came to 1.30 percent, about 26 US cents. [SOURCE: CGAP, 2017] [ESTIMATE — 1.3 percent of $20] [VERIFY]

The wallet operator's own margin on that same transaction was thinner still, around 0.5 percent. [SOURCE: CGAP, 2017] [VERIFY] A transaction that used the agent's counter space, cash, and time left the agent a margin smaller than the price of a cup of tea.

The customer side of that same $20 transaction shows why the older, informal system never fully went away. A customer paid 5.70 percent to send money the old-fashioned over-the-counter way, but only 1.85 percent to send the identical amount as a wallet-to-wallet transfer. [SOURCE: CGAP, 2017] [VERIFY]

The wallet was cheaper for the customer, but not for the provider. Formal over-the-counter transfers left the provider a gross margin of 3.75 percent, against just 0.5 percent on the wallet. [SOURCE: CGAP, 2017] [VERIFY]

CGAP's own reading of that gap was blunt: over-the-counter transfers were so much more profitable at the transaction level that it implied providers themselves had a vested interest in keeping customers on the costlier option rather than moving them onto the cheaper wallet the whole policy push was designed to promote. [SOURCE: CGAP, 2017] [ANALYSIS]

That thin margin creates a real, documented pull away from the wallet business entirely. CGAP separately found that an agent earns a commission every single time a customer sends money the old-fashioned over-the-counter way, but only once, when a customer first activates a wallet. [SOURCE: CGAP, "The EasyPaisa Journey from OTC to Wallets in Pakistan"]

Converting a customer onto the wallet permanently is in the operator's interest. It is not obviously in the agent's. [SOURCE: CGAP, "The EasyPaisa Journey from OTC to Wallets in Pakistan"] [ANALYSIS]

That incentive is not trivial money for some agents. CGAP found anecdotal evidence that target-based compensation for sending over-the-counter transactions can account for up to 60 percent of some agents' total mobile-money revenue, though CGAP itself is careful to label this anecdotal rather than measured. [SOURCE: CGAP, 2017] [ESTIMATE — CGAP's own anecdotal figure, not independently measured] [VERIFY]

None of that yet counts an agent's biggest daily headache: float, the pile of physical cash and digital wallet balance an agent has to keep sitting ready for the next customer on either side of the counter. Multi-country research, including surveys covering Pakistan, consistently names liquidity management as agents' single largest operating cost, though no research found in this pass isolates a precise Pakistan-specific share of that cost. [SOURCE: CGAP; Financial Inclusion Insights survey] [ANALYSIS] [VERIFY]

The tariffs a customer actually sees today follow the same shape as CGAP's decade-old benchmark. A 2026 accounting of published charges shows JazzCash charging Rs 35 to withdraw up to Rs 1,000, rising in tiers to Rs 400 on withdrawals between Rs 40,001 and Rs 50,000. [SOURCE: Pakistan Observer, 2026] [VERIFY — secondary aggregator, re-check against operator's own schedule]

Easypaisa's own tiers run from Rs 7 up to Rs 690 across that same range. [SOURCE: Pakistan Observer, 2026] [VERIFY] Neither operator's published tariff names a separate cash-in fee, consistent with cash-in staying a free, non-fee-bearing line on the customer-facing tariff. [ANALYSIS]

SBP's own regulation gives that tariff a floor of stability, if not a ceiling on price. Every wallet operator must publish its full fee schedule every quarter and lock it for the length of that quarter, unable to raise charges mid-quarter. [SOURCE: State Bank of Pakistan, Branchless Banking Regulations, 2019]

That rule exists because a fee an operator can move at will is a fee a customer can't budget around. SBP's answer to that risk is transparency and quarterly stability, not a hard price cap. [ANALYSIS]

On paper, then, an agent earns a small, regulated commission on each cash-in and cash-out, just enough to cover a sliver of the cost of holding liquidity for the next customer. The real question is whether that official number is actually what ends up in the till.

## Part 3 — How Agents Actually Keep More Than That

The commission structure in Part 2 assumes cash-in costs the customer nothing. That assumption holds as an industry norm, but not as a specific line SBP has written into regulation.

The 2019 Branchless Banking Regulations, read in full, contain no clause stating that cash-in must be free of charge to the customer. [SOURCE: State Bank of Pakistan, Branchless Banking Regulations, 2019] [VERIFY — no broader free-cash-in circular confirmed] The closest the regulation comes is requiring operators to publish, and lock, whatever fees they do charge.

How real is the free-cash-in norm in practice? SBP's 2021 circular required banks, microfinance banks, and e-money institutions to keep interbank fund transfers free to the customer up to Rs 25,000 a month, with only a small, capped fee allowed above that threshold. [SOURCE: Profit by Pakistan Today, 2021, describing the SBP circular] [VERIFY — primary circular text not independently opened]

After that waiver, an industry source told Profit by Pakistan Today that operators, trying to recover the lost revenue, might start charging customers for every cash-in at an agent counter instead. [SOURCE: Profit by Pakistan Today, 2021] [REPORTED]

The source itself called that move contradictory to the entire financial-inclusion mission. [SOURCE: Profit by Pakistan Today, 2021] That is a company-level worry about a possible future policy shift, not proof that any agent, anywhere, is already doing it. [ANALYSIS]

A separate, harder layer sits underneath that official structure: an arrangement some in the industry describe, where an agent routes a customer's cash-out through the agent's own personal wallet or bank account instead of the operator's official agent channel, treating it as an ordinary transfer between two people rather than a company-tracked agent transaction. [VERIFY — described by industry sources, not independently corroborated in this research]

Handled that way, the operator never takes its share of the commission, and the agent would be free to set whatever price they choose for handing over the cash. [VERIFY] [ANALYSIS] No Pakistani press investigation, SBP complaint record, or consumer-protection circular found in this research describes that specific mechanism actually happening. [VERIFY]

The same industry sources describe some agents charging a small fee for cash-in too, on a service the published tariffs treat as free. [VERIFY — not independently corroborated] That claim carries the identical caveat: no independent record of it was found either. [VERIFY]

The best available research actually complicates both claims rather than confirming them. CGAP's 2017 field study, built on agent interviews, mystery shopping, and central-bank data across Pakistan, Ghana, Tanzania, and Bangladesh, placed Pakistan specifically in what it called a "low informal OTC market." [SOURCE: CGAP, "Wallet and OTC Transactions: Understanding Financial Incentives," 2017]

Its own conclusion was that agents in the markets it studied, Pakistan included, don't typically have or use meaningful leverage to steer transactions off the books. [SOURCE: CGAP, 2017] That is a genuine complication for the workaround claim, not a footnote to explain away. [ANALYSIS]

It is also close to a decade old, finished before Raast existed and before the four-fold growth in wallet accounts that followed it. [ANALYSIS] It describes a market that may well have changed since, in ways this research could not independently re-test. [VERIFY]

What can honestly be said is this: the official commission model in Part 2 is the confirmed floor of what an agent earns, a workaround layer on top of it is described by industry sources but not proven, and the one rigorous study that went looking for it in Pakistan specifically didn't find much. [ANALYSIS] Whatever an agent actually nets at the counter, the pattern of where agents choose to operate at all is easier to test, and that pattern shows up clearly on a map.

## Part 4 — Where the Agents Are and Aren't

Density on the ground tracks the economics in Parts 2 and 3 almost exactly. As of the most recent published provincial breakdown, December 2023, Punjab held 413,991 branchless-banking agents, 63.72 percent of the entire national count. [SOURCE: Data Darbar Insights, analysis of SBP data, 2024] [VERIFY]

That is a bigger share of the country's agents than Punjab's share of the country's population. [ANALYSIS]

That share has also barely moved. In 2019, Punjab already held 62.99 percent of the country's agents — meaning four more years of stated financial-inclusion attention added less than one percentage point to Punjab's dominance. [SOURCE: Data Darbar Insights, 2024] [VERIFY]

That's not because growth elsewhere stalled; it's because Punjab kept adding agents just as fast as everywhere else. [ANALYSIS]

Measured per adult rather than in raw counts, the gap holds up just as sharply. Punjab had 516 agents for every 100,000 adults, against 188 in Balochistan, nearly three times the coverage. [SOURCE: Data Darbar Insights, 2024] [ESTIMATE — 516 divided by 188 ≈ 2.74] [VERIFY]

That is not a coincidence of geography. It is what Part 2's commission math predicts: an agent earning a fraction of a percent on every cash-in and cash-out needs constant transaction volume to make holding liquidity worthwhile, and volume concentrates where money already moves fastest. [ANALYSIS]

Transaction value backs that pattern up. Agents in Punjab moved Rs 11.06 trillion in 2023, against Rs 3.47 trillion in Sindh and Rs 2.7 trillion in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa. [SOURCE: Data Darbar Insights, 2024] [VERIFY]

The counterpoint is that the network is still spreading into the provinces it has mostly skipped, just far more slowly. Balochistan's agent count grew 27.1 percent a year between 2019 and 2023, and Gilgit-Baltistan's grew 27.5 percent, the two fastest growth rates in the country. [SOURCE: Data Darbar Insights, 2024] [VERIFY]

Both provinces are growing fast for the same reason Punjab is dense: off a base small enough that any real increase looks dramatic in percentage terms. [ANALYSIS] The absolute gap between 516 agents per 100,000 adults and 188 is not closing on any timeline visible in this data. [ANALYSIS]

Fewer agents competing for the same customers in a low-density market also means less competitive pressure on whatever an agent charges beyond the official tariff. If the workaround practices described in Part 3 turn out to be real anywhere, a thin, low-competition market is exactly where they'd be easiest to run without a customer noticing or complaining. [ANALYSIS] [VERIFY]

None of this required a regulator or a government office to decide rural Pakistan mattered less. A commission structure paid per transaction did the deciding on its own, the same way it always does in any business paid that way. [ANALYSIS]

But a network built to cluster where volume already exists has, without anyone planning it, built the perfect on-ramp for whatever comes next: a payment rail that can skip the cash step this entire counter economy depends on.

## Part 5 — The Rail That Could Replace the Cash Step

Raast is SBP's own instant-payment system, built to move money between any two bank accounts or wallets in seconds, for free, without cash changing hands at all.

In the quarter ending March 2026, Raast processed 742.1 million transactions worth Rs 23.3 trillion. [SOURCE: State Bank of Pakistan, Payment Systems Quarterly Review Q3 FY26, 2026] [VERIFY]

That is roughly five Raast transactions for every one transaction branchless-banking agents processed that same quarter, though most of Raast's volume is people paying people directly, not the cash-conversion business this episode has been tracing. [SOURCE: State Bank of Pakistan, Payment Systems Quarterly Review Q3 FY26, 2026] [ESTIMATE — 742.1 million divided by roughly 155 million agent-facilitated transactions] [VERIFY]

Most of that Raast volume is exactly that: person-to-person transfers, 664 million of them that quarter, up 10 percent, worth Rs 18.9 trillion. [SOURCE: State Bank of Pakistan, Payment Systems Quarterly Review Q3 FY26, 2026] [VERIFY]

The part of Raast built specifically to replace a shop counter is Person-to-Merchant payment, money moving straight from a customer's wallet to a shop's account. That volume grew from 36.3 million to 55.9 million transactions in a single quarter, with more than 2.6 million merchants registered on the system by quarter-end. [SOURCE: State Bank of Pakistan, Payment Systems Quarterly Review Q3 FY26, 2026] [VERIFY]

Fast-growing as that is, it's still a small slice of Raast's own P2P total the same quarter, about one Person-to-Merchant transaction for every twelve person-to-person transfers. [ANALYSIS] [ESTIMATE — 55.9 million as a share of 664 million P2P transactions, same quarter]

QR-code payments, where a customer scans a code at the till instead of handing over rupees, grew even faster: 87.3 million transactions, up 41 percent in a single quarter by volume and 63 percent by value. [SOURCE: State Bank of Pakistan, Payment Systems Quarterly Review Q3 FY26, 2026] [VERIFY]

The physical hardware hasn't caught up to any of that growth. Pakistan had just 247,836 point-of-sale machines across 217,042 registered merchants as of March 2026, even with 2.5 million merchant locations already registered for QR payments. [SOURCE: Startup.pk, 2026] [VERIFY]

That's roughly ten QR-registered locations for every working point-of-sale machine in the country, a rough comparison given the two datasets' different reporting dates, but a useful illustration of how far ahead the software has run of the hardware. [ANALYSIS] [ESTIMATE — 2.5 million QR locations divided by 247,836 POS machines ≈ 10] The rails exist well ahead of the machines that would let most small merchants actually use them.

That gap hasn't stopped the industry from being candid about where it wants this to go. An executive at one of the two dominant mobile-wallet operators has said publicly that cash itself needs to become more expensive than digital payments, to push customer behaviour away from it. [SOURCE: Business Recorder, 2026] [VERIFY — paraphrased, exact quote and speaker title not independently confirmed this pass]

That is a company built partly on agent commission income arguing, on the record, for making its own agents' core service less attractive to use. [ANALYSIS]

That's not just rhetoric. Telenor Microfinance Bank, which runs Easypaisa, earned Rs 6.3 billion in fee and commission income in 2020, more than its Rs 5.9 billion in interest income that year, while the bank posted a loss in both 2019 and 2020. [SOURCE: Profit by Pakistan Today, 2021, citing Telenor Microfinance Bank financial statements] [VERIFY]

A bank that already leans on fee income more than interest income, and still runs at a loss, has a direct financial stake in whichever side of the cash-versus-digital line ends up cheaper to serve at scale. [ANALYSIS]

SBP has already drawn that exact line for one large, policy-visible group of customers. Beneficiaries of the BISP cash-transfer program who withdraw their payment as physical cash through the new digital-wallet system now pay a Rs 280 fee, while an online transfer, or a Raast transaction, stays free. [SOURCE: ProPakistani/Business Recorder, 2026] [VERIFY]

SBP has said it will revise that fee schedule every six months. [SOURCE: ProPakistani/Business Recorder, 2026] [VERIFY] Free digital, paid cash, written directly into a national welfare program. [ANALYSIS]

None of that has yet shown up as a measured decline in the agent network's own business. In the same quarter Raast processed 742.1 million transactions, branchless-banking agents themselves still facilitated roughly 155 million transactions worth Rs 1.1 trillion. [SOURCE: State Bank of Pakistan, Payment Systems Quarterly Review Q3 FY26, 2026] [VERIFY]

No dataset or analyst report found in this research isolates a drop in agent volume caused specifically by Raast. [VERIFY — absence-of-evidence finding] The honest read is that the incentive and the growth curve both point one direction, cash made relatively less convenient and more expensive, digital made free and instant, but the volume data doesn't show the agent side shrinking yet. [ANALYSIS]

So the network Parts 1 through 4 described, urban-heavy, propped up by thin commissions and a workaround layer nobody can fully confirm, is now facing a rail explicitly built to remove its own reason for existing. What does the actual inclusion story look like once all of that sits on the table together?

## Part 6 — What This Means for the Inclusion Promise

Hold the achievement and the gap in the same frame, because both are real. The rise from single-digit financial inclusion to over a third of adults, described in Part 1, is a genuine structural change, and a shopkeeper's counter did most of that work. [ANALYSIS]

No bank-branch network in Pakistan's history reached this many new customers this fast. [ANALYSIS]

But the same commission economics that built that reach also drew its boundary. An agent whose income depends on transaction volume will always cluster where volume already exists, and the Punjab-versus-Balochistan density gap from Part 4 is what that boundary looks like on a map. [ANALYSIS]

The gender gap is harder to explain by commission economics alone. Part 1's finding, that only 14 percent of women are financially included against 56 percent of men, traces back further than any agent's location choice, to who owns a phone in the first place. [ANALYSIS]

The honest accounting of what an agent actually earns still has a hole in it, too. If the workaround layer described in Part 3 is real and widespread, actual agent income, and actual customer cost, both run higher than the official commission model shows; if it isn't, the official model is the whole story. [ANALYSIS]

This research could not close that gap either way, and the script has treated it as unresolved rather than picking a side to make a cleaner narrative. [ANALYSIS]

Layer the Raast threat from Part 5 on top of both findings, and the picture sharpens further. A rail that lets a customer skip the cash-conversion step entirely doesn't need to fix the agent network's geography or its unresolved workaround economy to matter. [ANALYSIS]

It can simply make both irrelevant, for anyone who already owns a smartphone and a bank-linked account. [ANALYSIS]

Awareness of Raast itself already repeats Part 1's pattern. Only 15 percent of adults report knowing what Raast is at all, and within that group, men are aware at nearly five times the rate of women, 24 percent against 5 percent, with the gap widest in rural areas. [SOURCE: Karandaaz Financial Inclusion Survey, 2024] [VERIFY]

A rail built to eventually replace the agent counter is, on this evidence, least known today to the exact population that counter still serves. [ANALYSIS]

That is the actual state of the inclusion promise today: real, large, unevenly distributed, partly undocumented at the edges, and increasingly running against a clock the agent network didn't set. What remains is what any of that means for the people and businesses who actually depend on this network continuing to work.

## Part 7 — The Verdict

None of what this episode has described points to a single villain or a single fix. Agent commission economics did their job: they spread mobile-wallet access faster than any bank-branch network in Pakistan's history, and they thinned out exactly where transaction volume was thinnest. [ANALYSIS]

That thinning was never a policy decision. It was arithmetic: a percentage-based commission naturally rewards volume, and volume was never evenly distributed across the country to begin with. [ANALYSIS]

The workaround layer this episode examined stays exactly where the evidence leaves it: described by industry sources, not proven, and complicated rather than confirmed by the one study that looked for it directly in Pakistan. [ANALYSIS]

Anyone repeating that claim as settled fact is going further than the research supports.

Raast is the part of this story most likely to actually change the picture from here. It doesn't need to out-compete agents on trust or convenience to matter; it only needs enough of the country to own a smartphone and a linked account for the cash-conversion step this entire commission model depends on to matter less. [ANALYSIS]

For the shopkeeper running an agent counter today, that points to one narrow, practical fact. The value of that counter is the ability to turn cash into digital money for someone who has no other way to do it, not the wallet transaction by itself. [ANALYSIS]

As Raast and QR payments spread, that ability holds exactly for the customer who still has no smartphone, no bank-linked account, or no comfort using either alone. That is a shrinking customer base by definition, but the numbers in Part 1 show it isn't a small one yet, particularly among Pakistani women, older customers, and rural households furthest from both a bank branch and a QR code. [ANALYSIS]

For a fintech operator or a policymaker, the practical test is the one Part 6 already set up. Does the next reform expand who this network reaches, or does it just push the network's fastest-growing customers onto a rail that no longer needs an agent at all? [ANALYSIS]

On the evidence gathered here, Raast is built to do the second thing whether or not anyone intends it to.
