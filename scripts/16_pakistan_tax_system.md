## Editorial frame

### Approved angle

How Pakistan's Tax System Actually Works — an institutional explainer arguing that Pakistan's tax system looks like an income-tax system on paper but functions like a transaction-tax system in practice, and that this design explains both the low official tax-to-GDP ratio and the high felt burden on the documented minority.

### Central question

How does money actually move from a Pakistani's pocket to the state, and why does so little of it end up as usable government revenue?

### Thesis

Pakistan's low tax-to-GDP ratio and its documented population's high felt burden are two symptoms of the same design: income tax is collected mostly through withholding rather than filed returns, indirect taxes do more revenue work than income tax, the 18th Amendment split federal and provincial tax authority in ways that let whole categories of income fall through, and the filer/non-filer system patches the resulting gap rather than closing it.

### Story logic

Institutional and structural. The episode follows the actual mechanics of collection — who withholds, who files, who is constitutionally allowed to tax what — rather than a chronology of policy events, and treats cash retail, agriculture, and real estate as predictable products of that mechanics rather than a separate mystery.

### Structure rationale

Seven parts, approved by the user. Part 1 establishes the anomaly. Parts 2 through 5 build the mechanism in the order money actually moves: which government has the legal authority to tax it (Part 2), how much of what's collected is indirect versus direct (Part 3), how the direct share is actually gathered — mostly withheld, barely filed (Part 4), and how the state tries to pressure the undocumented without ever seeing their real income (Part 5). Part 6 shows where that mechanism structurally cannot reach. Part 7 synthesizes without introducing new figures.

## Part 1 — The Anomaly

In 2023, Pakistan's government collected tax equal to 10.5 percent of the size of its economy. The average government in Asia and the Pacific collected 19.5 percent — almost double. [SOURCE: OECD, Revenue Statistics in Asia and the Pacific, 2025]

On paper, that looks like a country simply not collecting enough income tax. In practice, most of what Pakistan does collect never goes near a filed return. It is withheld from a salary, added to a phone bill, folded into a bank transfer, before the person paying it has any say in the matter.

This episode traces where that money actually goes. Which government has the legal right to collect it, how little of it comes from someone sitting down and filing a return, and why a documented, salaried Pakistani can feel taxed at every turn while the state, overall, collects so little.

A salaried employee's pay is taxed before it reaches their account.

A phone bill carries tax before the call connects. A bank transfer is taxed before it clears.

None of these people are choosing to pay. The money is gone before they see the total.

FBR's own numbers, published in October 2025, show its collection reaching 10.3 percent of GDP in the fiscal year that had just closed, up from 8.8 percent the year before. [SOURCE: FBR Revenue Division Year Book 2024-25, 2025]

Those two facts are not in tension. They are the same system, seen from two different heights.

A citizen who is documented — who has a bank account, a salary, a registered phone connection — gets taxed constantly and automatically. A transaction, a sector, an entire category of income that sits outside that documented structure can go largely untouched.

Pakistan's population passed 241 million in the 2023 census. [SOURCE: Pakistan Bureau of Statistics, 2023 Digital Census] The tax system was never built to reach all of them the same way, and it still doesn't.

So the question this episode answers isn't whether Pakistan's tax collection is low. That much is settled. It's how money actually moves from a Pakistani's pocket to the state, and why so little of it ends up as usable government revenue, even while so much is being taken automatically and constantly from the documented minority.

## Part 2 — Two Tax Systems Under One Roof

Start with a basic fact most viewers assume without ever checking. There is no single Pakistani tax authority.

There are two, operating under two separate constitutional mandates. Which one taxes a given rupee depends on what kind of rupee it is.

In 2010, Pakistan's parliament passed the 18th Amendment, a broad devolution package that reassigned a wide range of government functions from the federal government to the provinces.

On the tax side, the amendment left the Federal Board of Revenue — FBR, the federal government's revenue-collecting arm — in charge of income tax on everything except agricultural income, sales tax on goods, customs duty on imports, and federal excise duty on specific products like tobacco and cement. [SOURCE: Business Recorder, 2024]

Everything else moved to the provinces, or was confirmed as already theirs: capital gains tax and wealth tax on immovable property, capital value tax, the authority to legislate and collect their own sales tax on services, and agricultural income tax, which had constitutionally been a provincial subject all along. [SOURCE: Business Recorder, 2024]

Provinces then built their own machinery to collect the services tax they had just gained authority over. Sindh set up the Sindh Revenue Board in 2010. Punjab followed with its own Punjab Revenue Authority in 2012. Khyber Pakhtunkhwa created its own authority in 2013. [SOURCE: production research confirmation, 2026]

Balochistan came last. Its own sales tax on services only took effect in 2015. [SOURCE: TaxationPk, 2026]

Four provinces means four separate tax administrations, each defining and taxing a "service" on its own terms, and that has already produced friction. Punjab and Khyber Pakhtunkhwa tax a service where it is consumed; Sindh taxes it where it originates.

A company operating across both jurisdictions can end up billed twice for the same transaction, a dispute that has landed in litigation. [SOURCE: Dawn, 2024]

Underneath both systems sits a formula that decides how much money the federal government even has to spend, before provinces add their own collections on top: the National Finance Commission Award, or NFC Award.

The NFC Award sets how the federal government's shared pool of tax revenue, the divisible pool, is split between Islamabad and the four provinces.

The 7th NFC Award, signed in 2010, set the current split at 57.5 percent to the provinces and 42.5 percent to the federal government, a sharp jump from the roughly 49 percent provinces received under the prior formula. [SOURCE: 7th National Finance Commission Award, 2010]

Within that provincial share, a weighted formula splits the money further: 82 percent by population, with smaller weights for poverty, revenue effort, and low population density.

That formula produces a split of 51.74 percent to Punjab, 24.55 percent to Sindh, 14.62 percent to Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, and 9.09 percent to Balochistan. [SOURCE: 7th National Finance Commission Award, 2010]

Two further commissions have been convened since 2010. Neither produced a replacement award. The 2010 formula is still the one in force today. [SOURCE: production research confirmation, 2026]

That formula was fixed sixteen years ago, on a population and an economy that have both changed since. Revising it means one province accepting a smaller share so another can receive more, and no such change has been agreed since 2010.

Two separate administrations and one sixteen-year-old formula settle who has the legal authority to tax what. They don't explain why, between the two of them, so much of what actually gets collected comes from transactions rather than income.

## Part 3 — The Indirect Backbone

Ask most people how a government pays for anything, and the instinctive answer is income tax, tax on what people and companies earn.

In Pakistan, that instinct is close to wrong, and the gap between the assumption and the actual number is the clearest evidence of what kind of tax system this is.

FBR collected Rs 11,744.3 billion in total in FY2024-25, an average of roughly Rs 32 billion a day, every day, across every tax it runs. [SOURCE: FBR Revenue Division Year Book 2024-25, 2025] [ESTIMATE — Rs 11,744.3 billion divided across 365 days]

Of that, direct taxes, mostly income tax, made up 49.3 percent, or Rs 5,791.7 billion. Indirect taxes, collected on transactions rather than income, made up the other 50.7 percent, or Rs 5,952.6 billion. [SOURCE: FBR Revenue Division Year Book 2024-25, 2025]

For most of the last decade, that split was not close to even.

The Pakistan Economic Survey's own multi-year table shows direct tax's share of total FBR collection rising from 36.5 percent in FY2020-21, to 45.6 percent in FY2022-23, to 48.7 percent in FY2023-24, to today's 49.3 percent. [SOURCE: Pakistan Economic Survey 2025-26, 2026]

That is a real, sustained shift, confirmed independently by both FBR's own Year Book and the Ministry of Finance's own Economic Survey, which report identical figures for the same year.

As recently as five years ago, this would have been a very different episode. Indirect tax was doing nearly two-thirds of the work. It is now doing barely more than half.

That shift matters, but it shouldn't be read as Pakistan becoming an income-tax country.

Sales tax alone, charged on goods at the point of sale and ultimately paid by whoever buys the good, brought in Rs 3,901.4 billion in FY2024-25, 33.2 percent of everything FBR collected. [SOURCE: FBR Revenue Division Year Book 2024-25, 2025]

That single tax line is worth more than two-thirds of all direct tax collected the same year.

Customs duty on imports added another Rs 1,284.6 billion, 10.9 percent of the total. [SOURCE: FBR Revenue Division Year Book 2024-25, 2025]

Both taxes are collected the same way regardless of who is buying. A factory owner and a day laborer pay the identical sales tax rate on the identical bag of cement.

That is what makes indirect tax structurally regressive: it takes no account of ability to pay.

Provinces are collecting their own indirect tax on services, but at a fraction of the federal scale.

Combining what the four provincial revenue authorities reported collecting in sales tax on services and related levies in FY2024-25 produces a rough total of Rs 652.7 billion, an estimate calculated from each authority's own released figures, not an official combined statistic. [ESTIMATE — calculated from Sindh Revenue Board, Punjab Revenue Authority, Khyber Pakhtunkhwa Revenue Authority, and Balochistan Revenue Authority figures reported by Profit by Pakistan Today, Business Recorder, and Associated Press of Pakistan, 2026]

That is under a sixth of what FBR collected in sales tax on goods alone. The provinces gained taxing authority in 2010. They still haven't gained anything close to matching collection capacity.

That is the split between direct and indirect. It doesn't yet explain how the direct half actually gets collected, how much of it comes from someone filing a return, and how much never touches one.

## Part 4 — Income Tax Without Filing

Direct tax sounds like it should mean a person or company calculates what they earned, files a return, and pays what they owe.

In Pakistan, that is the smallest part of how direct tax actually gets collected.

Most of it is withheld. Withholding tax is deducted automatically the moment a transaction happens, by an employer paying a salary, a bank paying interest, a telecom company billing a call, an importer clearing goods at the port, and sent straight to FBR before the money ever reaches the person or business it belongs to.

In FY2024-25, withholding tax brought in Rs 3,381.5 billion, roughly Rs 9.3 billion a day. [SOURCE: FBR Revenue Division Year Book 2024-25, 2025] [ESTIMATE — Rs 3,381.5 billion divided across 365 days]

FBR's own breakdown states that this single mechanism accounted for 59 percent of all income tax collected that year, and 28.8 percent of everything the federal government collected across every tax it runs. [SOURCE: FBR Revenue Division Year Book 2024-25, 2025]

The rest of income tax splits two more ways, and both still stop short of someone choosing to file and pay.

Advance tax, payments made in installments through the year based on an estimate of what will be owed, made up another 33 percent.

Collection on demand, tax FBR had to extract through audits, notices, and enforcement action rather than receive voluntarily, made up 5 percent, itself up from 3 percent the year before. [SOURCE: FBR Revenue Division Year Book 2024-25, 2025]

Add those three together, 59, 33, and 5, and only about 3 percent of Pakistan's income tax collection is left over. [ESTIMATE — calculated as the remainder after withholding, advance, and enforcement shares from the same FBR Year Book]

That remainder is tax paid because someone filed a return and paid what it said they owed. Filing and paying against a self-assessed return, the act most people picture when they think of paying income tax, is close to the least common way Pakistan actually collects it.

Those withholding categories stop being abstract once they line up against a documented person's own year.

Salaries alone generated Rs 605.6 billion in withholding tax in FY2024-25, up 54.7 percent from Rs 391.4 billion the year before. [SOURCE: FBR Revenue Division Year Book 2024-25, 2025]

Contracts, payments to businesses and contractors, generated Rs 737.7 billion, up 39 percent. [SOURCE: FBR Revenue Division Year Book 2024-25, 2025]

Bank interest and securities added Rs 475.1 billion. Imports added Rs 421.8 billion. Electricity bills added Rs 144.4 billion. Telephone bills added Rs 123.4 billion. [SOURCE: FBR Revenue Division Year Book 2024-25, 2025]

Every one of those categories is a moment a documented Pakistani already recognizes from their own life: the payslip, the electricity bill, the phone bill, the bank statement. And every one of them is tax collected without the person doing anything that resembles filing.

This is why the low tax-to-GDP ratio and the high felt burden coexist without contradiction. The system isn't failing to tax documented people. It is taxing them constantly, at the point of every transaction they make inside the formal, banked, billed economy.

What it collects almost none of, within that same direct-tax total, is the one mechanism, the filed return, that would let FBR see and assess someone's actual total income.

The state has built a machine that is very good at intercepting money in motion, and comparatively poor at establishing what any one person or company actually made in a year. Closing that gap is exactly what the next part of the system was built to try.

## Part 5 — Filer, Non-Filer, and the Patch

If withholding tax collects money without ever confirming what someone earned, the obvious next question is what happens to people who stay entirely outside that documented structure: no salary on the books, no bank account generating withholding, no registered phone plan.

FBR's answer, built up over more than a decade, is the filer and non-filer system.

The distinction dates to the Finance Act 2014, which introduced higher withholding tax rates for anyone not on FBR's list of income tax filers, a premium for staying undocumented rather than a requirement to document. [SOURCE: Business Recorder, 2024]

That system has since been extended well past a rate difference.

The Tax Laws (Amendment) Act 2024, folded into the Finance Act for 2025, introduced a new provision, Section 114C. It defines an "eligible person" as someone who filed a tax return for the previous year and whose wealth statement shows resources that can plausibly justify the transaction they're making. Anyone who doesn't meet that bar is "ineligible." [SOURCE: Business Recorder, 2024]

The reported restrictions on ineligible persons are specific and material. Banks are to close accounts holding more than Rs 1 million and restrict new account openings. Property above a government-notified value can't be registered or transferred to them. They can't register or purchase a motor vehicle, or buy securities, including mutual fund units. [SOURCE: Business Recorder, 2024]

The stated goal, from government officials quoted in press coverage, is to broaden the documented tax base by making major transactions costly for anyone who stays outside it. [SOURCE: PropPakistani, 2025]

The honest complication is that this provision hasn't moved cleanly from law to enforcement.

The National Assembly's Standing Committee on Finance and Revenue deferred Section 114C in early 2025, after FBR's own chairman told the committee the department's systems weren't ready to support it. Banks and property registrars would need to check a person's filer status in real time, and that infrastructure wasn't yet built. [SOURCE: Business Recorder, 2025]

The provision was then carried into the Finance Bill for FY2025-26. [SOURCE: PropPakistani, 2025]

How far it is actually being enforced today isn't something the public record settles cleanly. Press coverage into 2026 describes banks, vehicle-registration offices, and property registrars checking a buyer's Active Taxpayer List status before completing a transaction, but no FBR enforcement statistic in this research confirms the scale at which transactions are actually being blocked. [VERIFY]

What the filer/non-filer system does, even in its most optimistic reading, is raise the cost of staying undocumented for someone making a large, traceable purchase.

It doesn't require that person to disclose their actual income, only that they've filed something and hold a wealth statement that isn't obviously implausible. A person can become a "filer" without FBR ever independently verifying what they earn.

The system taxes the transaction and gates a handful of major purchases behind documentation status. On the evidence available, it doesn't establish real income the way a properly assessed return would. That is a patch on the gap Part 4 described, not a repair of it.

And it still assumes the person shows up in the banked, documented economy at all: opening an account, registering a vehicle, buying property through a formal transfer. The next question is what happens to the money that never does.

## Part 6 — Where the System Stops

Cash retail, agricultural income, and undervalued real estate get discussed in Pakistan as three separate scandals. They aren't separate. They are the predictable edge of the exact mechanism already described.

Withholding tax works because there is an intermediary sitting between two parties in a transaction, a bank, an employer, a telecom company, a customs officer, who can deduct tax and hand it to FBR before the money moves.

A cash sale in an open market has no such intermediary. Nobody withholds anything, because nobody stands between the buyer and the seller.

That isn't a loophole exploited by any one shopkeeper. It is the structural boundary of a withholding-based system. Retail conducted in cash, outside the banking system, is largely invisible to the mechanism that raises most of Pakistan's income tax.

Agricultural income sat outside the system for a different reason: the constitution put it there.

As Part 2 established, agricultural income tax has always been a provincial subject, not a federal one. For decades, provinces taxed it lightly or not at all, even as agriculture remained a major share of the national economy.

That started to change only recently, and concretely.

Under IMF program conditionality, all four provinces were required to harmonize their agricultural income tax rates with federal personal and corporate rates, up to 45 percent for the highest earners, by an original deadline of end-October 2024.

Every province missed that deadline. Every province then passed the required legislation anyway, within the following few months: Punjab first, in November 2024, then Khyber Pakhtunkhwa on 27 January 2025, Balochistan on 3 February 2025, and Sindh on 4 February 2025.

Implementation began in January 2025; collection began in September 2025. [SOURCE: IMF Country Report No. 25/109, 2025]

A category of income the system had structurally excluded for decades is now, for the first time, inside the same rate structure as salaries and corporate profits.

Whether provinces can actually collect against that new law at scale is a separate question from whether the law exists, and it is too early in the collection cycle to answer it.

Real estate sits at a similar boundary: the value the tax system records for a property and the price it actually sells for aren't the same number.

FBR maintains its own notified valuation tables for major cities, revised periodically since 2016, meant to sit closer to real market prices than the older district-administration rates they supplemented.

Even after those revisions, industry reporting consistently describes FBR's notified values as still running well below what properties actually change hands for in most cities. [VERIFY]

The exact size of that gap is contested, and not independently confirmed by a primary government or academic study in this research, so it shouldn't be quoted as a precise multiple. Its direction isn't in dispute: a large share of Pakistani real estate is bought and sold at a documented value lower than its market price.

A transaction happens. The amount the state can see and tax is smaller than the amount that actually changed hands.

All three of these, cash retail, agricultural income until 2025, undervalued real estate, describe the same shape: money moving through the economy in ways the withholding-and-filing machine described in Parts 3 through 5 was never built to intercept.

Estimates of how much economic activity this leaves outside the documented system vary widely, from a third of GDP to nearly three-fifths, depending entirely on the methodology used. [VERIFY] No single figure in that range is settled enough to state as fact.

What is settled is the direction: a meaningful share of Pakistan's economy transacts in a form the tax system, as built, cannot see.

That is the real test behind every tax headline from here forward. Does closing one of these gaps actually change the two numbers this episode opened with, or does it just tighten the grip on the people the system can already see?

## Part 7 — The Verdict

None of what this episode has described is a single failure with a single fix.

It is a set of structural choices, indirect tax carrying half the revenue load, direct tax collected mostly through withholding rather than filing, taxing authority split between a federal board and four provincial ones, and a filer/non-filer system that raises the cost of staying undocumented without confirming anyone's real income, that together explain both numbers from Part 1: the low tax-to-GDP ratio, and the high felt burden on anyone already inside the documented, banked, salaried economy.

Reading a future tax headline against this structure changes what it means.

A new agricultural income tax collection figure isn't evidence the base has been fixed. It's a test of whether four separately administered provincial systems can actually collect against a law that took most of a year to legislate and has been running for less than one collection cycle.

A new super tax on large companies raises revenue at the margin, but it does so by taxing entities already fully inside the documented system. It doesn't touch the retail, agricultural, or real-estate activity that sits outside it.

An IMF benchmark on tax-to-GDP is a target on the federal number this episode opened with. It says nothing about whether an increase came from broadening who is taxed, or from raising rates on people already being withheld.

Real change would mean absorbing costs that have gone unaddressed since 2010.

It would mean renegotiating an NFC Award formula that hasn't moved in sixteen years, even as the population it was built for has grown by tens of millions.

It would mean FBR building the systems capacity its own chairman said the department lacked when Section 114C was deferred. Real-time verification is a precondition for a documentation-based reform to actually work, not a detail.

And it would mean provincial governments following through on agricultural income tax collection from large landholders, and federal and municipal authorities narrowing the remaining gap between a property's notified value and its market price.

Both of those mean taxing categories of income that have avoided full taxation for decades, not because the law never reached them, but because enforcing it against them has never actually happened.

None of that is a prediction about what will happen. It is a description of what would have to happen for the two numbers in Part 1 to move toward each other: for the tax-to-GDP ratio to rise without simply raising the burden on the same documented, salaried population already carrying most of it.

For founders, professionals, and investors trying to read Pakistan's tax policy going forward, that is the working test. Does a given reform expand who the system can see, or does it just tighten the grip on who it already sees?

Most of what gets called tax reform in Pakistan, on the evidence gathered here, has so far been the second kind.
