## SECTION 1 — The Unsolved Reality  (0:00 - 2:00)
*~280 words. On-the-ground entry point.*
*Rhythm: short punchy sentences. Echo a key word for weight. Name the anomaly before explaining it.*
*Transition: "That was not an accident." End by stating the episode's single question.*

Every year, Pakistani factories pack bales of cotton yarn onto ships at Port Qasim. A significant portion of those bales sail east to Bangladesh [VERIFY] [SOURCE: PBS trade data]. In Bangladesh, factories spin that yarn into shirts, trousers, and jackets for H&M, Zara, and Primark. Those garments then ship to Europe and North America.

The yarn leaves Pakistan worth roughly two to three dollars per kilogram [VERIFY] [SOURCE: APTMA / international cotton indices]. The finished garment made from it sells for fifteen to twenty dollars at factory gate [VERIFY] [SOURCE: BGMEA]. Bangladesh captures most of that difference. Pakistan captures almost none of it.

That is not a logistics problem. It is a value chain problem. And Pakistan has had it for forty years.

Pakistan is the world's fourth-largest cotton producer [VERIFY] [SOURCE: USDA]. It grows the raw material the entire global textile industry depends on. Bangladesh grows none. Vietnam grows none. Cambodia grows none. Yet all three countries earn more from finished garments than Pakistan does from its entire textile chain.

Pakistan's total textile exports last year were roughly 17 billion dollars [VERIFY] [SOURCE: PBS / SBP]. Bangladesh's were roughly 47 billion [VERIFY] [SOURCE: BGMEA]. Bangladesh, with no cotton fields, earns nearly three times what Pakistan earns from an industry built on Pakistani raw material.

That gap did not appear because Pakistan lacked the ability to make shirts. Pakistani factories make shirts. They make very good yarn. They make fabric. But at some point in the last four decades, the investment that should have gone into the next step of the chain — cutting, stitching, finishing, and building buyer relationships — went somewhere else.

Where it went, and why it cannot easily come back, is what this episode is about.

## SECTION 2 — The Paper Trail  (2:00 - 5:45)
*~530 words. PKR unit economics and financial anatomy.*

The textile value chain has five steps: cotton, yarn, fabric, garments, brands. Each step adds value. Each step requires more capital, more skill, and more time to master. And the further down the chain you go, the higher the margin.

Pakistan sits at steps one through three. It grows cotton, spins it into yarn, and weaves it into fabric. Those are the commodity end of the chain. Commodities are priced by global markets, not by the manufacturer. When global cotton prices fall, Pakistan's export earnings fall with them, regardless of how efficiently Pakistani mills operate.

Bangladesh sits at step four: garments. A garment is not a commodity. An H&M buyer placing a shirt order with a Dhaka factory is not just buying fabric — they are buying a production schedule, a compliance certificate, a quality guarantee, and a relationship with a factory that has delivered ten thousand orders without missing a deadline. That relationship has a price. Bangladesh has been building those relationships since the 1980s.

The margin difference is not marginal. A kilogram of cotton yarn sells on the global market for roughly two to three dollars [VERIFY]. A kilogram of finished shirts sells for fifteen to twenty dollars at factory gate [VERIFY] [SOURCE: BGMEA / APTMA]. The yarn in those shirts might cost three dollars. The garment earns twenty. The difference is manufacturing value addition — and it is where most of the employment, foreign exchange, and tax revenue actually comes from.

Pakistan's value addition per dollar of textile export is among the lowest in Asia [VERIFY] [SOURCE: World Bank / APTMA analysis]. Pakistan exports heavily in yarn and grey fabric — items that Bangladesh, India, and Vietnam then convert into finished products. It is the difference between selling crude oil and selling petrol.

Energy costs land directly on top of this. Pakistan's industrial electricity tariff has been among the highest in the region for most of the past decade [VERIFY] [SOURCE: NEPRA / regional tariff benchmarks]. A garment factory in Dhaka and a garment factory in Lahore making the same shirt pay very different electricity bills. The Lahore factory's power comes from a grid carrying the cost of the circular debt already covered in Episodes 06 and 07. The Dhaka factory's electricity is cheaper [VERIFY]. That cost difference arrives in the price the factory quotes to the buyer. The buyer picks the lower price.

Trade preferences add another layer. Bangladesh qualifies as a Least Developed Country under World Trade Organisation rules, which gives its exports duty-free access to the European Union under the Everything But Arms arrangement [SOURCE: European Commission]. Pakistan has GSP+ status — also preferential, but with more conditions and at lower margins [SOURCE: EU GSP+ scheme documentation]. When a European buyer is choosing between Bangladesh and Pakistan for the same order, the duty differential is not the deciding factor. But it is a factor.

Pakistan's garment share of total textile exports is roughly 30 to 35 percent [VERIFY] [SOURCE: APTMA / PBS]. Bangladesh's garment share is over 90 percent [VERIFY] [SOURCE: BGMEA]. That single comparison explains most of the earnings gap. Pakistan sells the material. Bangladesh sells the product.

## SECTION 3 — The Field Reality  (5:45 - 9:30)
*~530 words. Ground-level mechanics.*

The standard explanation for why Pakistan never moved up the value chain is energy. That is true but incomplete.

Energy costs are real. A garment factory — with air conditioning, precision cutting machinery, and the compliance systems that international buyers require — consumes far more power than a spinning mill. When electricity is expensive and unreliable, the economics of upgrading a spinning mill into a garment facility are harder to justify. That is a genuine constraint.

But Bangladesh in 1980 also faced difficult conditions. The country had just fought a liberation war. Infrastructure was minimal. Power was unreliable. The difference was not that Bangladesh had better conditions. It was that a specific group of entrepreneurs made a specific decision.

In 1979, a Bangladeshi businessman named Noorul Quader struck a deal with Daewoo, the South Korean manufacturer. He sent 130 Bangladeshi workers to South Korea to train in Daewoo's garment plants. They returned and set up Desh Garments in Dhaka [SOURCE: Mostafa Abid Khan, academic textile histories]. Within a decade, many of those trained workers left to start their own factories, taking what they had learned with them. Four million workers now make garments in Bangladesh [VERIFY] [SOURCE: BGMEA / ILO]. The industry grew from one joint venture and 130 workers.

Pakistan had its own opportunity in the 1980s. Spinning and weaving capacity grew as nationalization damage eased and private investment returned. But the investment went into the stages Pakistan already knew — yarn and fabric. Moving into garments meant dealing with a completely different kind of buyer.

European and American apparel brands require factory audits, environmental compliance, worker welfare certifications, and payment terms that can stretch 90 to 120 days from shipment. That is a fundamentally different business from selling yarn to a trading company. It requires management skills, outside expertise, and a willingness to open your operations to outside scrutiny. The Seth management model — Pakistan's dominant structure for family-owned industrial businesses, efficient at running a spinning mill with known margins and known buyers — is structurally resistant to that kind of scrutiny. Not from dishonesty, but from a culture built on the premise that what happens inside the factory stays inside the factory.

Foreign direct investment might have bridged that gap, the way Daewoo did in Bangladesh. It did not happen at scale. Pakistan's FDI environment — political instability, inconsistent regulation, expensive energy, and the difficulty of repatriating profits — has not attracted the manufacturing investors that Bangladesh, Vietnam, and Cambodia did.

The brands stayed away for the same reason. An apparel brand building a supply chain makes a five to ten year commitment to its manufacturing base. The brands that built in Bangladesh in the 1980s are still there. The brands that evaluated Pakistan and moved on did not leave because Pakistani workers cannot sew a shirt. They left because the energy cost, the compliance difficulty, and the operating environment made the risk-adjusted return unfavorable compared to the alternatives.

That assessment has not fundamentally changed. Pakistan's garment sector has grown, but not at the rate required to close a 30-billion-dollar gap.

## SECTION 4 — The Systemic Domino Effect  (9:30 - 12:30)
*~420 words. Macroeconomic impact.*

Missing the garment shift did not stay contained in the textile sector. It runs through Pakistan's economy at three levels.

The first is foreign exchange. Pakistan's current account — the balance between what the country earns from exports and what it spends on imports — has been in structural deficit for most of the past two decades [VERIFY] [SOURCE: SBP balance of payments]. Bangladesh runs a far more resilient current account because garment exports provide a large, steady dollar inflow [VERIFY] [SOURCE: Bangladesh Bank]. Every dollar Pakistan does not earn from garment exports is a dollar it must borrow, attract through remittances, or cover with IMF support. Pakistan's recurring IMF programs are not separable from the recurring shortfall in its export earnings.

The second is employment — specifically, the kind of employment that compounds. Bangladesh's garment workforce is predominantly women, many from rural backgrounds, entering formal employment for the first time [VERIFY] [SOURCE: BGMEA / ILO]. That employment changed household income, female labor participation, and demographic patterns across Bangladesh in ways that accumulate over generations. Pakistan has an informal textile and garment sector — small workshops in Faisalabad, Multan, and Karachi — but informal employment does not build the skills base, the worker protections, or the management depth that formal factory work does. And it does not appear in FBR's documentation net, which leads directly to the third level.

Tax revenue. Formal garment factories pay corporate tax, sales tax, and withholding tax on an auditable basis. Pakistan's small-scale garment and embroidery sector operates largely below FBR's threshold. The tax base stays narrow. The fiscal deficit stays wide. That fiscal deficit is what drives the borrowing, the debt, and the IMF conditions that are the subject of every second headline about Pakistan's economy.

The deeper consequence is exposure to commodity cycles. An economy that exports yarn and fabric rises and falls with global cotton prices. Bangladesh, selling finished garments at negotiated prices, is insulated from that cycle. It can absorb a drop in raw material costs as a margin improvement. Pakistan cannot — a drop in cotton prices compresses the margin on the only thing Pakistan is selling. The volatility in Pakistan's export earnings is not random. It is the direct result of sitting at the commodity end of the chain.

## SECTION 5 — The Verdict & Future Outlook  (12:30 - End)
*~340 words. Realistic forecast and concrete implications.*

That commodity exposure, and the foreign exchange shortfall that comes with it, will not change until Pakistan's garment share of textile exports changes. The two are the same problem.

The gap between Pakistan and Bangladesh is not closable in a policy announcement. It is a 40-year head start in buyer relationships, compliance systems, workforce skills, and supply chain infrastructure. Pakistan cannot replicate that on a short timeline. What it can do is stop falling further behind.

The most direct lever is energy. A competitive industrial electricity tariff — which depends on resolving the DISCO privatisation and circular debt structure the last two episodes covered — would directly reduce the cost disadvantage Pakistani garment manufacturers face. This is achievable within five years if the structural reforms are executed. It is not achievable through subsidy alone.

The second lever is compliance infrastructure. International brands do not avoid Pakistan because of its workers. They avoid it because factory audit and compliance in Pakistan is expensive, inconsistent, and sometimes unreliable. Sector-wide compliance programmes — the model Bangladesh built jointly between government and BGMEA — exist in Pakistan's context as an unfinished project under APTMA [VERIFY] [SOURCE: APTMA compliance reports]. Without scale and consistency, buyers will not make long-term commitments.

The third lever is targeted FDI in the cutting and making stages specifically — not spinning, not weaving, but the downstream steps where Pakistan currently earns least. A Bangladeshi or Vietnamese garment manufacturer setting up in Lahore would bring buyer relationships, compliance systems, and management expertise that cannot be built domestically in the short term. Pakistan needs to be the most attractive option for that investment, which means the energy, the regulation, and the operating environment all have to be competitive at the same time.

Bangladesh did not build its industry by having better geography or better workers. It built it by being the most reliable option available to international buyers at a critical moment. That moment passed for Pakistan forty years ago. A second one is not guaranteed. But the raw material is still growing in the fields of Punjab and Sindh. The question is whether Pakistan builds a factory around it this time.
