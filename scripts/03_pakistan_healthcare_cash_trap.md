## SECTION 1 — The Unsolved Reality  (0:00 - 2:00)
*~300 words.*

[VISUAL 00:00 — a hospital cashier window at night. A man counts out a thin stack of banknotes, pauses, and pulls a pair of gold bangles from his pocket to make up the rest. Hold on his face.]

A family walks into a hospital with a sick child. Before anyone treats the child, someone has to pay. There is no insurance card to hand over. There is no company or government that quietly picks up the bill. There is only cash. And if the cash is not enough, the family sells something. Gold. A motorbike. Sometimes a piece of land.

[VISUAL 00:30 — a simple line of text builds on black: "In Pakistan, you mostly pay for healthcare with your own money."]

This is how most healthcare in Pakistan is paid for. Not by insurance, not by the state, but straight out of a family's own pocket. And that one fact quietly turns getting sick into one of the fastest ways to become poor.

[VISUAL 01:00 — a large number counts up and holds: "~11,000,000 people".]

Here is the number that should stop you. In a single year, an estimated eleven million Pakistanis were pushed below the poverty line for one reason: medical bills [SOURCE: World Bank analysis, 2024] [VERIFY]. Not floods, not job losses. Just the cost of getting treated. About one in every eight households spends so much on health that it eats into food, rent, and school fees [SOURCE: catastrophic-health-expenditure studies, 2018-19].

[VISUAL 01:35 — title card on black: "ONE ILLNESS AWAY", green accent on "ONE".]

So how did healthcare become a thing that can ruin you? In this brief, we follow the actual rupees a sick family spends, we go to the pharmacy and the private clinic to see where that money really goes, and we work out why a country of 240 million people never built the one thing that would protect them: insurance.

## SECTION 2 — The Paper Trail  (2:00 - 6:00)
*~580 words.*

[VISUAL 02:00 — a clean pie chart begins to draw itself, ready to be filled in.]

Let us start with the money, because the money explains everything.

Add up everything Pakistan spends on health in a year, from the government, from hospitals, from families, all of it. It comes to roughly three percent of the country's total income, its GDP [SOURCE: WHO / Commonwealth Fund, 2022] [VERIFY]. That is low. Similar countries spend closer to five and a half percent [SOURCE: WHO regional data, 2022]. So the first problem is simple. As a country, Pakistan does not spend much on health to begin with.

[VISUAL 02:40 — a bar labelled "PAKISTAN ~3% of income" next to a taller bar "SIMILAR COUNTRIES ~5.5%".]

Now, who actually pays inside that small pool? Here is the part that matters. The government covers only a slice, historically less than one percent of the country's income [SOURCE: Commonwealth Fund; PULSE, 2021-2022] [VERIFY]. So who covers the rest? Families do. About half of all health spending in Pakistan comes straight out of people's own pockets [SOURCE: Commonwealth Fund; out-of-pocket studies, ~50-54%].

[VISUAL 03:20 — define-on-screen card slides in: "OUT OF POCKET = money you pay yourself, with no insurance or government help."]

"Out of pocket" just means money you pay yourself, in cash, with nobody sharing the cost. In most countries, insurance or the state carries the big medical bills so no single family gets crushed. In Pakistan, the family carries it alone.

[VISUAL 03:50 — the pie chart fills: a large red slice "MEDICINES ~51%", then "DOCTOR FEES ~13%", "TESTS ~8%", "TRANSPORT ~8%".]

And where does that pocket money go? The biggest single cost is not the surgeon or the hospital bed. It is medicine. More than half of what families spend out of pocket goes on medicines alone [SOURCE: out-of-pocket composition study]. Then doctor fees, then lab tests, then just getting to the hospital. So the everyday cost of being sick in Pakistan is, above all, the cost of pills.

[VISUAL 04:40 — a simple household example builds: "Family income Rs50,000/month" with a chunk peeling off for "diabetes medicine, every month, forever".]

Picture a normal family earning fifty thousand rupees a month. One member develops diabetes or high blood pressure, a lifelong illness. Now there is a medicine bill every single month, forever, and it never goes away. Add one hospital admission, and the bill can swallow a month's income or more in a matter of days [SOURCE: NCD out-of-pocket studies] [VERIFY]. There is no salary that was designed to absorb that.

[VISUAL 05:00 — a simple contrast: in one country a person hands over an insurance card at the hospital desk; in Pakistan, a person opens a wallet.]

In most of the world, this is exactly the moment insurance steps in. You hand over a card, and the big bill is spread across thousands of other people who paid into the same pool. In Pakistan, there is usually no card to hand over. The whole national health pool, government and families together, is only about ten billion dollars a year, and the government pays under forty percent of it [SOURCE: Commonwealth Fund, 2022] [VERIFY]. The rest lands on families, one emergency at a time.

[VISUAL 05:20 — a "Sehat Card" graphic appears, then a stamp lands on it: "INPATIENT ONLY".]

Now, the government has tried to help with health cards, the Sehat Sahulat programme. A card can cover big hospital bills, up to around four hundred thousand rupees, and as much as a million in some provinces [SOURCE: Sehat Sahulat Program, 2024-2025]. That is real. But read the fine print. The card mostly covers "inpatient" care, meaning when you are actually admitted to a hospital bed [SOURCE: Sehat Sahulat Program]. The everyday medicine bill, the biggest cost of all, is usually still paid in cash. So the paper trail leaves us with a sharp question. The single largest expense is the one the safety net does not catch.

## SECTION 3 — The Field Reality  (6:00 - 10:00)
*~580 words.*

[VISUAL 06:00 — inside a busy neighbourhood pharmacy. People at the counter describing symptoms; the pharmacist handing over strips of tablets. No doctor in sight.]

To see how this really works, do not go to a hospital. Go to a pharmacy. Because for millions of Pakistanis, the pharmacy is the first doctor, and often the only one.

[VISUAL 06:30 — a person at the counter mimes a headache; the pharmacist hands over a few loose tablets, not a full box.]

A doctor's visit costs money you may not have. So people skip it. They walk into the chemist, describe the problem, and buy medicine directly [SOURCE: self-medication and access studies]. And because cash is tight, they often do not buy the full course. They buy a few tablets at a time, whatever today's money allows, and stop when it runs out. Pharmacists describe patients returning half-used boxes, treatments left unfinished because the money finished first [SOURCE: Dawn, 2024]. That is what poverty looks like inside a healthcare system. Not a dramatic scene, just a course of antibiotics that never gets completed.

[VISUAL 07:20 — a split screen: left, a crowded, bare public hospital; right, a clean but cash-only private clinic.]

Now, why not just use a free government hospital? Because the public system is stretched thin. It is overcrowded and often short of the very medicines you need. So even people with very little end up in the private system, where you pay for everything. More than eighty percent of that out-of-pocket money flows to private providers, not public ones [SOURCE: out-of-pocket private/public split study]. Pakistan effectively runs two health systems side by side. A private one that works, if you can pay. And a public one that is free, but so under-resourced that it often cannot deliver.

[VISUAL 07:50 — a person at a stall run by an unlicensed "doctor"; a hand-written list of medicine prices with the numbers scribbled higher.]

Because money is so tight, care gets rationed in quiet, dangerous ways. People delay treatment until a small problem becomes a big one. They turn to untrained practitioners, sometimes just a man with a shop and a box of injections, because he is cheaper than a real clinic. And the price of medicine itself keeps climbing, partly because the government loosened its control over drug prices in recent years [SOURCE: medicine price deregulation studies, 2024-2025] [VERIFY]. So the cheapest option, the pharmacy, keeps getting more expensive, and the poorest keep cutting their doses to cope.

[VISUAL 08:15 — a departures board style graphic: silhouettes of doctors leaving, a counter ticking up.]

There is another crack in the ground beneath all of this, and it is getting wider. The doctors are leaving. By official counts, around five thousand doctors emigrated in just two years, and a large share of each new batch of graduates settles abroad [SOURCE: Bureau of Emigration; medical-association data, 2024] [VERIFY]. Pakistan trains them, often with public money, and then exports them. Fewer doctors at home means longer queues, higher fees, and even more pressure on families already paying cash.

[VISUAL 09:00 — a family scene: a father borrowing money from a relative; a woman handing over gold bangles at a jeweller.]

So when a real emergency hits, the family does what the system quietly forces them to do. They borrow from relatives. They sell the wife's gold, which in many homes is the only real savings there is. They take a loan at brutal interest. The medical bill does not just cost money. It eats the family's savings, their assets, and sometimes their future. This is the field reality the clean numbers never show. A healthcare system that runs, day to day, on a family's willingness to sell what little it owns.

## SECTION 4 — The Systemic Domino Effect  (10:00 - 13:00)
*~450 words.*

[VISUAL 10:00 — pull back from one family to a wide map, thousands of small household icons lighting up.]

Now zoom out, because millions of families making this same choice add up to a national problem.

[VISUAL 10:30 — a poverty line graphic: a bar at "20.5%" rises to "27%" when a red block labelled "medical bills" is added.]

Start with poverty itself. On paper, about one in five Pakistanis lives below the poverty line. But once you count the money families lose to medical bills for long-term illnesses, that share jumps to more than one in four [SOURCE: NCD catastrophic-expenditure study]. That is roughly thirteen million extra people, made poor not by being jobless, but by being sick. Health spending is quietly one of the biggest poverty machines in the country.

[VISUAL 11:15 — a household budget shrinking: a plate of food getting smaller as a medicine bill grows.]

And it does not stop at money. When a poor family has to buy medicine, something else has to give. Studies show medical bills crowd out food and other basics, and the hit is worst for the poorest homes [SOURCE: NCD out-of-pocket crowding-out study]. So a diabetes diagnosis in one member can mean less food on the table for everyone. The illness spreads through the household budget.

[VISUAL 12:00 — a simple loop diagram: "sick → sell assets → poorer → weaker → sick", turning.]

Step back further and you can see a trap forming. A health shock makes a family poorer. A poorer family eats worse and works less, which makes it more likely to get sick again. Multiply that across millions of homes and it becomes a drag on the whole economy, a workforce that is more indebted, less healthy, and less productive than it should be.

[VISUAL 12:35 — a squeezed government budget graphic: a small pie with slices for debt and defence, and a tiny sliver for health.]

And the government cannot easily spend its way out. Pakistan collects very little in tax, and a large part of what it does collect goes to paying off old debt. Living under tight limits set by its lenders, the state has little room to pour money into hospitals and medicine [SOURCE: analysis of fiscal constraints on health spending, 2024] [VERIFY]. So the doctors keep leaving, families keep paying cash, and health spending stays stuck near the bottom of the budget. Cheap health policy today quietly becomes an expensive economy tomorrow, in lost work, lost people, and lost potential.

## SECTION 5 — The Verdict & Future Outlook  (13:00 - End)
*~360 words.*

[VISUAL 13:00 — clean slate, one question: "CAN THIS BE FIXED?"]

So what is the honest forecast here?

[VISUAL 13:20 — the "Sehat Card" graphic returns, half a shield drawn over a hospital but not over a pharmacy.]

The health cards are genuine progress, and they should not be dismissed. For a family facing a huge hospital bill, they can be the difference between coping and collapse. But they mostly cover the hospital, and the thing that bankrupts families day to day is the medicine bill outside the hospital, which the card usually does not touch. It is half a shield.

[VISUAL 13:55 — a simple animation of insurance: many people drop small coins into one shared pot; one sick person draws from it.]

The deeper missing piece is insurance itself, in its simplest form. Insurance just means many people each put a little into a shared pot, so that the unlucky few who fall sick can draw from it without being ruined. Pakistan has almost none of this for health. Until that changes, and until the government spends meaningfully more, the system stays exactly what it is today: pay cash, or suffer.

[VISUAL 14:10 — three small icons appear in a row: a phone (telemedicine), a shared-pot (micro-insurance), a pharmacy chain.]

The encouraging part is that the fix does not have to start with a giant government hospital. The same phone that reached almost everyone can carry a cheap doctor consultation. Small, low-cost insurance plans, sold for the price of a few cups of tea a month, can spread a family's risk. Organized pharmacy networks can bring medicine prices down through scale. None of this is science fiction, and other developing countries have started down exactly this road. Pakistan's advantage is that it is starting from so far behind that even simple fixes would matter enormously.

[VISUAL 14:30 — return to the opening cashier window; this time show a wider shot of a long queue of families, each waiting to pay.]

Here is the verdict. This is not really a story about hospitals. It is a story about risk, and who carries it. Right now, the answer is: the family, alone, at the worst possible moment. That is the real disease in the system.

[VISUAL 14:50 — three quiet takeaway lines build, then cut to black and the end card.]

For founders, the biggest open market in Pakistan may be low-cost health insurance, pharmacy networks, and telemedicine that reach people the hospitals never will. For employers, health cover is one of the most valuable things you can give a worker in this economy. And for anyone reading the country's future, watch two numbers: how much the state spends on health, and whether coverage ever reaches the pharmacy counter. That is where the real fight is.
