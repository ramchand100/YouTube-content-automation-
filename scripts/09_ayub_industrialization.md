## SECTION 1 — The Unsolved Reality  (0:00 - 2:00)
*~280 words. On-the-ground entry point.*
*Rhythm: short punchy sentences. Echo "22 families" for weight. Name the anomaly before explaining it.*
*Transition: "That speech was not an accident." End by stating the episode's single question.*

In 1968, Pakistan's chief economist gave a speech. He stood before a planning conference and announced, with statistics, that 22 families controlled 66 percent of Pakistan's industrial assets, 70 percent of its insurance, and 80 percent of its banking [VERIFY] [SOURCE: Mahbub ul Haq, published economic writings].

The economist's name was Mahbub ul Haq. He worked for the government of Ayub Khan. He was, in effect, indicting his own employer.

Twenty-two families. Not a sector. Not a class. Twenty-two specific families — the Saigols, the Dawoods, the Habibs, the Adamjees — who in the space of one decade had become the commanding architecture of Pakistan's entire private economy.

That speech was not an accident. It was the moment when the contradiction at the heart of Ayub Khan's economic model became too large to ignore.

The decade before had looked, by every available measure, like a success. Pakistan's economy grew at roughly six percent per year through the 1960s [VERIFY] [SOURCE: World Bank historical national accounts]. The World Bank called Pakistan a model for developing nations. Harvard economists flew in to advise the Planning Commission. New factories rose across Karachi and Lahore. New roads, new dams, new industries.

But the growth had a shape. It flowed upward and westward. East Pakistan — which held the majority of Pakistan's population and generated most of its foreign exchange through jute exports — watched Karachi and Lahore industrialise while Dhaka waited [SOURCE: Hamoodur Rahman Commission].

Mahbub ul Haq's speech named what the growth statistics had obscured. The question this episode asks is how Pakistan built one of Asia's fastest-growing economies in a single decade — and why that growth planted the seeds of everything that followed.

## SECTION 2 — The Paper Trail  (2:00 - 5:45)
*~530 words. PKR unit economics and financial anatomy.*

The industrialisation that produced those 22 families did not emerge from the market. It was engineered — deliberately, systematically, by a state that decided who it wanted to build and gave them the tools to do it.

The mechanism was industrial licensing. Starting a factory in 1960s Pakistan required a government licence. The licence determined what you could produce, at what scale, for which market. It was not allocated by auction or by competition. It went to families with capital, with commercial networks, and with relationships inside the civil service departments that issued them. The Saigols, who built the Crescent textile empire in Lyallpur, had those relationships. So did the Dawoods, the Adamjees, and the Habibs — families who had arrived from India at Partition with commercial experience and some capital, and who moved quickly into the industrial space that the new country's licensing system opened [SOURCE: Hanna Papanek, "Pakistan's Big Businessmen," Asian Survey, 1972].

The government then provided the credit. PICIC — the Pakistan Industrial Credit and Investment Corporation — was established in 1957 with World Bank backing to channel long-term financing into private manufacturing [SOURCE: PICIC official history]. The Industrial Development Bank of Pakistan served smaller projects. Together they made industrial capital available at rates the open market would not have offered. The risk was partly socialised. The profit was entirely private.

Import substitution completed the structure. Pakistan imposed high tariffs on imported consumer goods — textiles, sugar, basic chemicals, paper — to make domestic production competitive against cheaper foreign alternatives. A cotton fabric mill in Karachi did not need to be efficient. It only needed to be cheaper than the taxed import. The tariff wall was the competitive advantage, not the factory floor.

The result was exactly what it looked like. GDP grew at roughly six percent per year through the 1960s [VERIFY]. Manufacturing as a share of GDP expanded. New industrial cities grew around Karachi's port, Lahore's commercial base, and the Lyallpur textile belt. Pakistan built Tarbela and Mangla dams. The Green Revolution in agriculture, driven by new wheat varieties and fertiliser access, pushed rural incomes up in Punjab [VERIFY] [SOURCE: Pakistan Economic Survey historical].

What the growth rate did not show was where the surplus went. The licensing system concentrated opportunity. The credit system concentrated access to capital. The tariff system concentrated market share. Every mechanism reinforced the same small group. By 1968, when Mahbub ul Haq made his calculation, the concentration was mathematical fact: the machinery of Pakistani industry had been assembled in the hands of 22 families over the course of one decade.

## SECTION 3 — The Field Reality  (5:45 - 9:30)
*~530 words. Ground-level mechanics.*

The families were not passive recipients of state favour. They worked. They competed with each other, sometimes viciously, for licences, credit allocations, and market share. The Saigols expanded from yarn into weaving and then into energy. The Dawoods moved from textiles into chemicals. The Adamjees built the largest single jute mill in the world at Narayanganj in East Pakistan [VERIFY] [SOURCE: Adamjee Industries corporate history].

But the system they operated in had a structural problem that no amount of individual enterprise could fix. It excluded almost everyone else.

A trader in Peshawar with savings and ambition could not easily obtain an industrial licence. A farmer in East Bengal with cash from jute sales could not access PICIC credit. The civil service networks that allocated opportunity were concentrated in West Pakistan, among Punjabi and Urdu-speaking families with connections to the Pakistan Administrative Service. That was not a conspiracy. It was a geography of power — the capital was in Karachi, the bureaucracy was in Karachi and Rawalpindi, and the licences were issued by the bureaucracy.

East Pakistan felt this most directly. The province produced more than half of Pakistan's jute — the crop that earned most of the country's foreign exchange through the 1950s and 1960s [VERIFY] [SOURCE: Pakistan Economic Survey historical]. That foreign exchange was spent developing industry in Karachi and Lahore. East Pakistanis watched the transfer and kept account.

By the late 1960s, East Pakistani economists had a name for the arrangement: internal colonialism. The numbers supported the label. Development spending per capita in East Pakistan was significantly lower than in West Pakistan through most of the Ayub decade [VERIFY] [SOURCE: Planning Commission allocation data]. The civil service was predominantly West Pakistani. The military was predominantly West Pakistani. The 22 families — almost all of them West Pakistani — were the visible face of what the arrangement produced.

The mass movement that ended Ayub Khan's rule in 1969 was the convergence of these grievances. East Pakistani anger at economic exclusion. Urban workers in Karachi and Lahore facing inflation that the growth statistics did not capture. Students angered by the gap between Pakistan's model-economy reputation and their own prospects. And a political left that Mahbub ul Haq had handed a very specific number to work with. Twenty-two families. The slogan required no explanation.

Ayub Khan resigned in March 1969 and handed power to General Yahya Khan. The political crisis that followed — the 1970 election, the refusal to transfer power to the Awami League, the military crackdown, the liberation war — ended with Pakistan losing East Pakistan in December 1971. The factory owners of Karachi and Lahore survived that rupture. The Adamjees lost their largest mill. Most of the others regrouped in a smaller country and waited to see what the new government would do.

What Bhutto's government did to them is the subject of Episode 07. The relevant point here is that the 22 families survived nationalisation, adapted, and remain at the core of Pakistan's industrial economy today — a continuity made possible by the depth of the networks the Ayub decade created.

## SECTION 4 — The Systemic Domino Effect  (9:30 - 12:30)
*~420 words. Macroeconomic impact.*

The legacy of Ayub-era industrialisation runs forward through every economic structure Pakistan operates today — not as history but as a living architecture.

Import substitution was never fully dismantled. Pakistan still operates high tariff walls on a range of consumer and intermediate goods — textiles, automobiles, electronics. Those walls protect industries built in the 1960s behind protection and never forced to compete on efficiency. They also raise costs for downstream manufacturers and consumers. Pakistan's automobile sector — built on Ayub-era import substitution logic and never genuinely liberalised — sells cars to Pakistani consumers at prices well above comparable regional markets [VERIFY] [SOURCE: PAMA / regional car price benchmarks]. The 1960s industrial architecture is still collecting rent.

The concentration of ownership was never reversed. Bhutto's nationalisations targeted the commanding heights — steel, cement, banking — but the textile and trading families largely survived, dispersing capital into real estate and trading as Episode 07 described. Pakistan's large-scale manufacturing today is still dominated by a small number of family groups who trace their origins directly to the licensing era. Their names appear in SECP filings, on stock exchange boards, and in the boardrooms of the companies that received CPEC industrial zone allocations in the 2010s.

The CPEC industrial zones replicated the basic structure of the 1960s almost exactly: government-allocated land, subsidised infrastructure, preferential access for selected investors. The mechanisms that allocated opportunity in Ayub's time — bureaucratic relationships, political proximity, access to state-backed credit — did not disappear. They were updated.

The East Pakistan rupture left a structural consequence that is still felt. Bangladesh's emergence as an independent economy, covered in the previous episode, is partly the story of a population that had cross-subsidised West Pakistan's industrialisation for two decades and then built its own industrial base under different conditions. The jute revenues that funded Karachi's factories in the 1960s were gone after 1971. Pakistan's foreign exchange position — chronic, recurring, IMF-dependent — dates in part to the loss of that export base and the failure to replace it.

The civil service-industrial complex that Ayub built also persists. Industrial policy in Pakistan continues to be made through relationships between the bureaucracy, the military establishment, and large family groups rather than through transparent market mechanisms. The structure mutated. The logic did not.

## SECTION 5 — The Verdict & Future Outlook  (12:30 - End)
*~340 words. Realistic forecast and concrete implications.*

That persistent logic — relationships over markets, protection over competition, concentration over distribution — is the most durable inheritance of the Ayub decade. Not the factories themselves, which have aged and many of which have been nationalised, privatised, restructured, or abandoned. The logic.

Ayub Khan's decade produced something genuine. The factories were real. The growth rate was real. The dams were real. Pakistan in 1969 was a more industrialised economy than Pakistan in 1958, and that industrialisation formed the base on which everything subsequent was built, for better and worse.

But the model had a ceiling built in from the start. Import substitution creates industry behind a tariff wall. That industry can grow for as long as the domestic market grows and the tariff holds. It cannot export, because it was never built to compete internationally. It cannot easily innovate, because the tariff removes the competitive pressure that forces innovation. And it cannot be reformed politically, because the families it created become powerful enough to defend the system that made them.

South Korea faced the same ceiling in the early 1970s — its own import substitution phase had produced similar industrial concentration, similar family conglomerates, similar questions about equity. South Korea's response was to pivot: force its conglomerates to compete in export markets, withdraw protection from those that could not, and use the state to push industry up the value chain rather than simply to protect it at the bottom. The result was Samsung, Hyundai, POSCO.

Pakistan's pivot window was the 1970s. It spent that decade nationalising what the 1960s had built, which is why Episode 07 exists as a subject.

The window has not permanently closed. Pakistan still has industrial capacity, a large domestic market, and a diaspora that holds capital and skills. But the question Mahbub ul Haq was really asking in 1968 — not just how concentrated the wealth was, but whether the model that concentrated it could produce a broadly prosperous country — has not yet been answered in the affirmative.

Fifty-seven years later, it is still the right question.
