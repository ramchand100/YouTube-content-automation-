## SECTION 1 — The Unsolved Reality  (0:00 - 2:00)
*~300 words.*

[VISUAL 00:00 — tight shot at a small shop counter: a faded Raast QR sticker taped beside the till, curling at the edges. A customer pays with cash. The cashier barely glances at the sticker.]

Look at almost any shop counter in Pakistan and you will find a small sticker with a QR code on it. It is a doorway to Raast, the country's national instant payment system. It is free. It is instant. The State Bank built it. And most of the time, nobody uses it.

[VISUAL 00:25 — overlay two numbers side by side, animating up: "P2P: Rs11.3 TRILLION" next to "P2M: Rs17 BILLION".]

Here is the strange part. In a single quarter of 2025, Raast moved roughly twelve point eight trillion rupees [SOURCE: SBP via Profit by Pakistan Today, Q1 FY26, 2025]. People send money to each other on it constantly. It processes more than a trillion rupees person to person every nine days [SOURCE: SBP data, 2025] [VERIFY]. But money paid to merchants, the payments you would make buying groceries or clothes, was only about seventeen billion rupees in that same quarter [SOURCE: SBP, Q1 FY26, 2025]. That is a rounding error next to the person-to-person figure.

[VISUAL 01:05 — a card being swiped through a POS machine; then a stack of banknotes sliding across a counter. Label: "STILL THE DEFAULT".]

So a free, instant, government-built rail exists for paying shops. And the country still reaches for cash, or a card that quietly charges a fee, to move its own money.

[VISUAL 01:35 — title card on black: "WHY PAKISTAN PAYS TO PAY", green accent on "PAYS".]

This is not a story about a clever app. It is a story about incentives. In this brief, we follow the actual rupees through a card transaction, we go to the counter to see why merchants resist free money, and we work out who really loses when payments go free. The answer is not the one the marketing suggests.

## SECTION 2 — The Paper Trail  (2:00 - 6:00)
*~570 words.*

[VISUAL 02:00 — clean explainer canvas. A single receipt: "PURCHASE: Rs2,000". This one transaction gets dissected for the whole section.]

Start with the money. Follow one ordinary purchase, two thousand rupees at a retail shop, and watch what each way of paying actually costs.

[VISUAL 02:20 — the Rs2,000 receipt with a card. A slice peels off labelled "MDR ~2% = Rs40".]

Pay with a card, and the shop does not receive the full two thousand rupees. It pays a merchant discount rate, the MDR, for accepting the card. In Pakistan that discount has typically run around two percent [SOURCE: payment-gateway pricing, simpaisa / Rapid Gateway, 2024-2025] [VERIFY]. On a two thousand rupee sale, that is about forty rupees gone before the shopkeeper sees a paisa.

[VISUAL 02:55 — the Rs40 slice splits into three labelled pieces: "INTERCHANGE → issuing bank", "SCHEME FEE → Visa / Mastercard", "ACQUIRER margin".]

Now follow that forty rupees. The largest piece, the interchange fee, goes from the acquiring bank to the bank that issued the customer's card. In Pakistan that interchange has ranged from roughly half a percent to nearly one point eight percent, which is why the State Bank has moved to cap it near half a percent [SOURCE: Pakistan payment-system analysis, 2024]. A second piece, the scheme fee, goes to the card network itself. And here is the detail that matters for Pakistan. If that card runs on Visa or Mastercard, those network fees are effectively dollar-denominated and leave the country [SOURCE: analysis of international vs domestic card economics, 2024] [VERIFY]. For a country permanently short of dollars, that is real leakage on a domestic purchase.

[VISUAL 03:45 — a contrast panel: "VISA / MASTERCARD → fees exit in USD" vs "PayPak → fees stay in PKR, domestic only".]

There is a local alternative. PayPak, the domestic card scheme launched by 1LINK in 2016 under the State Bank, charges lower local fees and keeps the money inside Pakistan, though it only works domestically [SOURCE: 1LINK / PayPak, 2016 onward]. Cheaper, but still not free.

[VISUAL 04:20 — the same Rs2,000 receipt, now paid via Raast QR. A near-flat line: "COST ≈ Rs0".]

Now pay the same two thousand rupees over Raast. The cost is close to zero. It settles bank to bank, instantly, with no MDR carved out of the sale [SOURCE: SBP Raast, 2021 onward]. On paper, this is not a close call. Card, about forty rupees of friction on a two thousand rupee sale. Raast, effectively nothing.

[VISUAL 04:45 — a merchant's daily card sales counter spins up: "Rs100,000/day in card sales", then "MDR ~Rs2,000/day", then "~Rs60,000/month".]

Put that in a shopkeeper's terms. Take a mid-sized retailer doing one hundred thousand rupees a day in card sales. At roughly two percent, that is about two thousand rupees a day handed to the payment chain, close to sixty thousand rupees a month, for the simple act of being paid [SOURCE: derived from typical MDR, 2024-2025] [VERIFY]. Most of that goes to the bank that issued the customer's card, a smaller slice to the network, and whatever is left to the acquirer running the machine. Over Raast, that same volume moves for essentially nothing. For a business running on thin retail margins, sixty thousand rupees a month is not a rounding error. It is a salary.

[VISUAL 05:00 — a giant multiplier animation: "x billions of transactions". Then a number ticker for national retail payments.]

Multiply that gap across a country that ran nine point one billion retail digital transactions worth six hundred and twelve trillion rupees in a single year [SOURCE: SBP, FY2024-25] [VERIFY], and the amount of friction sitting inside the card model is enormous.

[VISUAL 05:35 — the Rs3.5bn subsidy graphic: coins flowing from "STATE" to "MERCHANT", tagged "0.5% or Rs100 per QR sale".]

And yet the government is not sitting back and letting the cheaper rail win on its own. In September 2025 it launched a subsidy of about three and a half billion rupees, paying merchants roughly half a percent, or one hundred rupees, on each Raast QR sale to get them to accept it [SOURCE: SBP, 2025]. Read that carefully. The free option is so unused at the counter that the state is paying people to take the cheaper deal. The paper trail says Raast wins easily. The market says otherwise. That gap is the real subject of this brief.

## SECTION 3 — The Field Reality  (6:00 - 10:00)
*~575 words.*

[VISUAL 06:00 — back to the shop counter, now with the shopkeeper as the focus. Two ledgers sit under the till.]

To understand why the free rail loses at the counter, you have to stop looking at the fee and start looking at what each payment leaves behind.

[VISUAL 06:25 — a cash note drops and vanishes with no trace; a digital payment drops and leaves a glowing record labelled "VISIBLE TO FBR".]

Cash leaves no trail. A digital payment, whether card or Raast, leaves a record. For a large share of Pakistani retail that operates partly or fully undocumented, that record is the real cost, not the two percent. A documented sale is a sale the Federal Board of Revenue can see, and being seen can mean tax, questions, and exposure the cash economy has spent decades avoiding [SOURCE: analysis of documentation and the cash economy, 2024-2025]. Measured against that, the MDR is almost beside the point. Many merchants are not refusing Raast because it costs too much. They are wary of it because it costs too little friction to the taxman.

[VISUAL 07:15 — Rs7 trillion currency-in-circulation graphic, captioned "~13% of GDP in cash".]

This is not a fringe habit. Pakistan carries more than seven trillion rupees of currency in circulation, around thirteen percent of GDP sitting as physical cash [SOURCE: reporting on currency in circulation, 2025] [VERIFY]. Card penetration has stayed thin: fewer than fifty million cards in a country of well over two hundred million, and by one measure a card here is used at a point of sale only about three and a half times a year, against more than sixty times in the European Union [SOURCE: Data Darbar / SBP data, 2022-2025] [VERIFY]. The counter runs on cash because the whole system around it does.

[VISUAL 07:35 — a supply chain: distributor hands goods to shopkeeper against a cash bundle; shopkeeper sells to customer for cash. The whole chain glows "CASH" end to end.]

The cash preference also runs up the supply chain, not just at the till. A typical retailer buys stock from a distributor in cash and sells it in cash. The moment sales start flowing through a documented rail, the mismatch between recorded sales and undocumented purchases becomes visible, which drags the supplier into the light too. So the resistance is not one shopkeeper being stubborn. It is an entire cash chain that only holds together as long as no single link gets documented [SOURCE: analysis of retail cash chains, 2024] [VERIFY].

[VISUAL 08:00 — a clock/settlement graphic: card money arrives "T+1, minus fees"; cash and Raast arrive "instantly".]

There is a cash-flow angle as well. Card settlement is not instant. The money typically lands in the merchant's account a day or more later, net of fees, while cash is liquidity in hand the moment the sale closes. For a small business managing daily working capital, that timing difference is not trivial. Raast, to its credit, settles instantly, which is one genuine edge it holds over cards on pure mechanics.

[VISUAL 08:05 — shift to a bank building. Arrows show "INTERCHANGE FEE → bank income" glowing green.]

Now add the second field reality, and this one is rarely said out loud. Banks earn money on cards. Interchange is fee income. Raast is free, which means a bank that aggressively pushes merchants onto Raast is cannibalizing its own card revenue [SOURCE: analysis of bank incentives in payments, 2024]. The acquiring business, signing up merchants and running POS machines, already runs on thin or even negative margins [SOURCE: Pakistan payment-system analysis, 2024]. So the institutions that distribute payment tools have a muted incentive to promote the one rail that earns them nothing.

[VISUAL 08:55 — a QR sticker being handed to a shopkeeper who shrugs; a POS terminal and a bank relationship stack up beside it.]

Layer these together and the counter starts to make sense. The distributor of the tool, the bank, would rather sell you a card machine. The user of the tool, the merchant, often does not want the visibility that any documented payment brings. And the customer, reaching for habit, pulls out cash or the card they already carry. The QR sticker on the counter is not failing because the technology is bad. It is losing to a set of incentives that all point the other way. That is the field reality the clean paper math never captures.

## SECTION 4 — The Systemic Domino Effect  (10:00 - 13:00)
*~450 words.*

[VISUAL 10:00 — pull back from the counter to a national view: money flows lighting up across the country.]

Zoom out, and this counter-level standoff becomes a national one.

[VISUAL 10:30 — dollars peeling off card transactions and flying out of the map, tagged "FX LEAKAGE".]

Start with foreign exchange. Every purchase run over an international card network sends a scheme fee out of the country in dollars. For an economy that lurches between dollar shortages and IMF programs, paying foreign networks to move domestic rupees is a slow, structural leak [SOURCE: analysis of card FX costs, 2024] [VERIFY]. A domestic rail like Raast, or even PayPak, keeps that money at home.

[VISUAL 11:05 — a tax-base graphic: a small "DOCUMENTED / SALARIED" pool taxed hard, a vast "CASH ECONOMY" pool barely touched.]

Then the tax base. As long as retail runs on cash, the economy stays undocumented, and the state collects far less than it should. So it leans harder on the people it can see, the salaried and the formal sector, because the cash economy is simply invisible to it [SOURCE: documentation and tax-base analysis, 2024-2025]. Raast, by making digital payment free and easy, is quietly a documentation tool as much as a payments tool. That is exactly why it is both promising and resisted.

[VISUAL 11:35 — the SBP building with a policy-rate dial; a large pool of cash sits outside the banking system, greyed out and unreachable.]

Cash outside the banking system also blunts monetary policy. When the State Bank moves its policy rate, that signal travels through the banking system. Trillions of rupees circulating as physical cash, outside deposits and outside the formal rails, sit largely beyond the reach of that transmission [SOURCE: analysis of cash and monetary transmission, 2024] [VERIFY]. A more digital, more documented payment system is not only a tax question. It makes the central bank's main lever work better.

[VISUAL 11:50 — a leapfrog graphic: a landline crossed out, a mobile phone rising, relabelled "cards → instant rails".]

There is an opportunity buried in this too. With card penetration below ten percent of the population [SOURCE: SBP data, 2022] [VERIFY], Pakistan may never build a card-heavy economy at all. It could leapfrog straight from cash to instant digital rails, the way it skipped landlines and went to mobile. If that happens, the winners and losers of the next decade of Pakistani banking get redrawn, because a bank's card-fee income stops being safe.

[VISUAL 12:30 — a consumer at a till: a small "+2%" tag floating over marked-up prices.]

And the ordinary consumer sits at the end of every one of these dominoes. Where merchants accept cards and absorb the MDR, they tend to bake it back into prices. Where they only take cash, the customer stays outside the digital and credit system entirely. Either way, the cost of a clogged payment system lands, quietly, on the person at the counter.

## SECTION 5 — The Verdict & Future Outlook  (13:00 - End)
*~360 words.*

[VISUAL 13:00 — clean slate. A single question on screen: "WILL THE FREE RAIL WIN?"]

So what is the realistic forecast, without the hype?

[VISUAL 13:15 — a simple two-line chart: P2P rising steeply, P2M rising slowly off a near-zero base.]

Free and instant payment rails tend to win eventually, and the region has already shown it can happen at scale [SOURCE: regional instant-payment adoption, 2020s] [VERIFY]. Raast's person-to-person growth is proof the demand is there. But merchant adoption, the part that actually documents the economy, will not be won by the rail being cheaper, because it is already free. It is stuck on something the technology cannot fix by itself.

[VISUAL 13:50 — three friction blocks stack up: "TAX VISIBILITY", "BANK FEE INCOME", "HABIT & INFRASTRUCTURE".]

Three frictions decide the pace. First and largest, the tax-visibility problem: as long as being documented is treated as a penalty, merchants will avoid the rail that documents them. Second, bank incentives: the institutions distributing payments earn nothing on Raast, so they will not carry it for free forever. Third, plain habit and infrastructure. The three and a half billion rupee subsidy is the clearest signal of all. The state knows adoption will not come from economics alone, so it is paying to move behavior.

[VISUAL 14:30 — the two ledgers from Section 3 return, relabelled "DOCUMENTED ECONOMY" vs "CASH ECONOMY".]

Here is the honest verdict. Raast will keep winning person-to-person payments quickly, and it will grind forward at the merchant counter mostly at the speed of policy, not technology. Because the real contest was never Raast against the card. It is the documented economy against the cash one, and payments are just where that fight is visible.

[VISUAL 14:55 — cut to the opening shop counter, the QR sticker now scanned, a phone screen showing a completed Raast payment. Hold, then to black and end card.]

For founders, build QR-first and assume the rails are free. For banks, a fee model resting on card interchange is living on borrowed time. And for anyone trying to read Pakistan's economy, watch the merchant-adoption curve and the tax rules around it. That number, not the app downloads, is the real signal.
