## SECTION 1 — The Anomaly

In 1988, Pakistan Railways carried 12 million tonnes of freight. Twelve million tonnes: grain, cement, coal, petroleum, fertilizer. The physical weight of a functioning economy, moving by rail. [SOURCE: CEIC / Ministry of Finance Pakistan, Railway Statistics, 2024]

By 2014, that figure had fallen to one million tonnes. One million. On the same tracks. In the same country. [SOURCE: CEIC / Ministry of Finance Pakistan, Railway Statistics, 2024]

That decline was not a coincidence.

Pakistan Railways reports 8.2 million tonnes of freight for fiscal year 2024-25, a partial recovery from that 2014 low. [SOURCE: Pakistan Railways / APP, 2026] [VERIFY] But the tonnage figure obscures what has actually happened. Pakistan Railways once handled 73 percent of all freight traffic in this country. [SOURCE: PIDE / academic literature] By 2011, that share had fallen below four percent. [SOURCE: PIDE / academic literature] Today, roads carry more than 94 percent of every tonne that moves across Pakistan. [SOURCE: World Bank / UNCTAD / PBC, 2020-2023]

A railway that once dominated national freight is now a marginal carrier. That status did not arrive by accident.

Every year that gap persists, manufacturers, farmers, and consumers in Pakistan pay more for the goods they make and the goods they buy. Rail freight is structurally cheaper per tonne-kilometre than road freight. Pakistan is paying the difference every day, built into prices that do not need to be this high.

Why did Pakistan Railways effectively exit the freight business, and what has that cost this economy?

## SECTION 2 — The Paper Trail

The financial anatomy of Pakistan Railways makes the freight collapse intelligible. Start with the revenue.

In fiscal year 2023-24, Pakistan Railways earned Rs 88.8 billion in total revenue. [SOURCE: Pakistan Railways / multiple media, July 2024] Freight contributed Rs 28 billion, or 31.8 percent. Passenger services contributed Rs 47 billion, more than half of all revenue. [SOURCE: Pakistan Railways / media reports, July 2024] A national freight carrier that earns more from passenger tickets than from moving goods is, in practice, a passenger carrier that also moves some freight.

The cost side explains why that imbalance is locked in. Salaries account for roughly 33 percent of Pakistan Railways' total operating expenses. Pension payments account for roughly 35 percent. Fuel accounts for approximately 21 percent. Those three items together absorb roughly 89 percent of everything Pakistan Railways spends. [SOURCE: Budget/media analysis, 2023-24] [VERIFY: year reference requires confirmation; figure attributed to FY2022-23 or FY2023-24 analysis] Eleven percent, on paper, remains for track maintenance, wagon repairs, locomotive overhauls, and capital development on a network that has received no major capital injection in four decades.

The pension bill tells its own story. The federal government allocated a Rs 55 billion grant to Pakistan Railways in fiscal year 2023-24, primarily to cover pension obligations for retired staff. [SOURCE: Media reports, 2024] [VERIFY] That pension grant is nearly twice the railway's entire freight revenue. In structural terms, Pakistan Railways is a pension fund that operates a transport service on the side.

The investment asymmetry is how this situation was produced and perpetuated. In the federal budget for fiscal year 2026-27, the National Highway Authority received Rs 224.5 billion in Public Sector Development Programme funding. Pakistan Railways received Rs 40.7 billion. [SOURCE: Budget FY2026-27, press reports, June 2026] [VERIFY] More than five to one in a single budget year. Replicate that ratio across two decades of annual budgets and the physical infrastructure gap becomes visible: a highway network that has been systematically expanded and maintained, and a railway mainline that still operates on track built to standards set in the 1960s.

Pakistan Railways' operating ratio for fiscal year 2023-24 stood at approximately 98 percent. [SOURCE: Pakistan Railways / APP, 2024] An operating ratio of 98 percent means the railway earns just enough revenue to cover its operating costs, leaving essentially nothing for reinvestment from internal resources. Every capital project requires a fresh federal allocation. Every capital decision is therefore political. That dependence is the mechanism by which forty years of underinvestment became structurally self-reinforcing.

## SECTION 3 — The Field Reality

The financial portrait explains the structure. Three specific events explain how cargo physically moved off the railway.

The first was a wheat crisis in 1977. Millions of tonnes of grain sat at Karachi port. Pakistan Railways could not move it fast enough. The army intervened, and the intervention produced the National Logistics Cell, NLC, a military-affiliated freight operator created to solve an emergency. [SOURCE: NLC official history / academic sources] NLC did not dissolve once the emergency passed. It grew. It secured preferred government contracts, expanded into petroleum, containerised cargo, and bulk commodities, and by 2011 was responsible for approximately 50 percent of Pakistan's domestic crude oil transport. [SOURCE: Wikipedia / NLC history, citing company reports] [VERIFY] NLC's freight volume is currently estimated at three times that of Pakistan Railways. [SOURCE: NLC / business journalism sources] [VERIFY: date of claim unconfirmed] A state-backed competitor with preferred institutional access does not displace a state carrier through market efficiency. It displaces it through institutional means.

The second event was the pipeline expansion of the 1990s. Pakistan Railways had historically moved petroleum products from Karachi inland as one of its most reliable cargo streams. When the pipeline network expanded to cover major inland routes in the 1990s, that traffic moved off rail permanently. [SOURCE: PIDE / academic literature] A commodity lost to a pipeline does not return to a train.

The third was a fuel crisis in 2000. A severe shortage of high-speed diesel left Pakistan Railways unable to operate its locomotive fleet. Freight collapsed. Shippers who built road alternatives during that period did not return to rail when supplies normalised. The practical cost of reorienting supply chains back to rail (different loading facilities, different warehousing, different scheduling) exceeded the theoretical freight rate savings for most operators. [SOURCE: PIDE / academic literature]

The result is a trucking sector that now moves, by volume, almost all national freight. Pakistan's registered truck fleet numbers approximately 300,000 vehicles. [SOURCE: PBC Research, Pakistan Road Logistics Sector] [VERIFY: publication vintage unclear; data may reflect 2015-2019 figures] The sector is overwhelmingly informal, mostly single-truck operators, with no central dispatch and no cold-chain standardisation. But there is genuine flexibility: trucks go door to door, leave when cargo is ready, and serve the dispersed manufacturing geography of Pakistan in ways a fixed rail network cannot.

That flexibility is real. For short-haul, mixed-cargo, door-to-door delivery, trucks have genuine advantages over rail that no policy can fully eliminate. But the evidence does not support the claim that market logic alone explains a freight tonnage decline of more than 90 percent between 1988 and the 2014 low. The severity of that collapse, which was far greater than comparable lower-middle-income economies experienced over the same period, reflects the compounding of NLC's institutional displacement, the petroleum pipeline loss, the fuel crisis, and four decades of capital starvation, on top of genuine market advantages for road freight.

## SECTION 4 — The Systemic Domino Effect

The freight collapse does not stay inside Pakistan Railways' balance sheet. It radiates outward through four channels.

The first is the direct logistics cost premium. Pakistan's logistics costs are estimated at approximately 35 percent above the South Asian regional average. [SOURCE: World Bank / FPCCI policy brief, attributed] [VERIFY: primary document not directly confirmed] Transport sector inefficiencies are estimated to cost the economy between four and five percent of GDP annually. [SOURCE: Pakistan Infrastructure Implementation Capacity Assessment, attributed] [VERIFY] One assessment puts the production cost overhead at approximately 30 percent above what it would cost to manufacture equivalent goods in a more efficiently connected regional economy. [SOURCE: Government/World Bank policy documents, attributed] [VERIFY]

The World Bank's Logistics Performance Index is a direct measure of the gap. Pakistan ranked 68th globally in 2016. By 2018, it had fallen to 122nd, one of the steepest two-year drops recorded in that index. [SOURCE: World Bank Logistics Performance Index] In 2023, Pakistan was excluded from the LPI entirely, due to insufficient data quality and inadequate stakeholder engagement. [SOURCE: World Bank Logistics Performance Index, 2023]

The second channel is road damage. Pakistan's freight trucks routinely carry loads above legal axle weight limits. Road damage scales with the fourth power of axle weight, meaning a small overload causes structurally disproportionate pavement deterioration. The cost of that damage falls on the highway maintenance budget, the same budget the National Highway Authority uses for new construction.

The third is road safety. The World Health Organisation estimates more than 27,000 people die on Pakistan's roads each year. [SOURCE: WHO estimate] The annual economic cost of road fatalities and serious injuries is estimated at approximately twelve billion US dollars, equivalent to roughly three percent of GDP. [SOURCE: Asian Transport Observatory / Tribune, 2021] [VERIFY: primary study not directly accessed] Heavy freight vehicles are a disproportionate contributor to that toll.

The fourth is export competitiveness. Pakistan's textile and agricultural sectors compete internationally on price. A logistics premium of 30 percent above regional norms compounds directly into export price disadvantage. A Pakistani textile exporter shipping to European buyers competes against manufacturers in Bangladesh, India, and Vietnam, all operating on lower logistics overhead. The freight cost gap does not disappear because wages are lower. It subtracts from the margin that would otherwise make Pakistani exports competitive.

## SECTION 5 — The Verdict

The path to reversing Pakistan's freight disadvantage is visible on paper. Executing it requires three things to happen simultaneously: capital investment in infrastructure, institutional reform of Pakistan Railways itself, and a competitive rebalancing that addresses the structural advantages the road-freight sector has accumulated over four decades. None of those three has been achieved to the required scale. All three remain politically constrained.

The most discussed capital intervention is ML-1, the modernisation of the 1,872-kilometre main line from Karachi to Peshawar. The project, approved at approximately USD 6.8 billion, would raise freight speeds to 120 kilometres per hour and increase line capacity from 34 to 171 train movements per direction per day. [SOURCE: International Railway Journal / Ministry of Railways; ML-1 project documents / media, 2025] [VERIFY: ADB rephasing may have revised the cost figure] The Ministry of Railways' economic viability study attributes a projected freight cost reduction of approximately 30 percent to the completed project. [SOURCE: Ministry of Railways economic viability study, attributed] [ESTIMATE: primary document not directly accessed; treat as working estimate until confirmed]

ML-1 matters. It is not the whole answer.

A modernised main line does not resolve Pakistan Railways' institutional cost structure. Salaries, pensions, and fuel currently absorb roughly 89 percent of operating expenditure, leaving almost nothing for reinvestment from internal resources. The pension obligation alone requires approximately Rs 55 billion per year in federal grant. That liability cannot be cleared by laying better track. Pakistan Railways shed 20,000 staff in early 2025 under IMF programme pressure, bringing its workforce to approximately 58,680. [SOURCE: Media / Railway reform reports, 2024] [VERIFY] Further labour reform is politically constrained in an institution where employment has historically been managed as a patronage resource, not a commercial input.

The trucking sector that displaced rail now employs millions across an informal economy with significant political weight. Any policy that meaningfully redirects freight to rail will reduce trucking employment. That is not an abstract tradeoff. It is a real political obstacle.

For manufacturers, exporters, and logistics investors operating in Pakistan today, the freight cost premium is a persistent structural input that should be priced into every capital and pricing decision. ML-1, if fully funded, built, and operated to its design parameters, changes the economics of bulk freight on the main corridor over a ten-to-fifteen year horizon. [ESTIMATE] The reversal of Pakistan's freight disadvantage is structurally possible. It is not imminent. The decisions that created the gap took four decades to accumulate. The decisions required to close it will take at least as long to execute, and require a political consistency in infrastructure investment that has not yet been demonstrated.
