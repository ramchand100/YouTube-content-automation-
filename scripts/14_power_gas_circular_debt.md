## Editorial frame

### Approved angle

The Machine That Never Stops Growing (widened from the originally approved Gas-Power Doom Loop angle) — Pakistan built its power system on a contract structure that pays for capacity whether or not it's used, layered a distribution system that doesn't collect what it's owed on top of it, wired the result into the gas sector's own finances, and has spent three decades renegotiating the edges of that structure without ever touching its foundation.

### Central question

Why does Pakistan's circular debt keep growing, through capacity-payment contracts, distribution losses, a linked gas sector, and trillions of rupees in claimed reforms, and what would it actually take to stop it?

### Thesis

The debt has one root architecture (take-or-pay capacity payments from the 1994 Power Policy) and two amplifiers (distribution-side non-payment and a gas sector wired to absorb the power sector's shortfalls); every reform round since has renegotiated individual contracts without changing that structure, which is why the debt keeps returning even after each round claims trillions of rupees in savings.

### Story logic

Structural and causal, in combination. The take-or-pay contract is the structural foundation; the way non-payment cascades through distribution companies, K-Electric, and into the gas sector is a causal chain built on top of it.

### Structure rationale

Five parts, approved by the user. Part 1 opens on the paradox of billions spent and the debt still growing, and states the central question. Part 2 explains the fixed-cost contract mechanism the whole system rests on. Part 3 shows who fails to pay into that mechanism. Part 4 shows how that failure spreads into a second sector. Part 5 tests the thesis against three decades of reform attempts and closes with a specific, honest verdict.

## Part 1 — The Machine That Never Stops Growing

In one year, Pakistan's government poured more than 300 billion rupees into subsidies for its power sector. [SOURCE: The News, 2026] [VERIFY]

That same year, the sector's unpaid debt still grew by 364 billion rupees. [SOURCE: Express Tribune, 2026] [VERIFY]

This episode looks at why. Not one villain, but a fixed-cost contract from three decades ago, a distribution system that doesn't collect what it's owed, a gas sector wired into the same shortfall, and a string of reforms that keep missing the actual problem.

Add up everything Pakistan's power and gas companies are owed and can't collect, and the number is 5.286 trillion rupees. [SOURCE: The Nation, 2026] [VERIFY]

That is roughly two and a half times the 2.1 trillion rupees Pakistan spent in a single year just on capacity payments, the fixed bill you'll meet in a moment. [ANALYSIS: Claude's calculation, 5.286 trillion divided by 2.1 trillion] [SOURCE: IEEFA, 2024] [VERIFY]

Split the total in two. The power sector alone owes 1.675 trillion rupees. [SOURCE: The News, 2026] [VERIFY]

The gas sector owes even more, 3.611 trillion rupees. [SOURCE: The Nation, 2026] [VERIFY]

Neither number has been standing still. Both have been growing for years.

So which is it. A power problem. A gas problem.

Or the same problem, wearing two different names.

Every year, the government finds new subsidies, new loan guarantees, new committees to fix this.

Every year, the number gets bigger anyway.

So why does this debt keep growing, no matter what gets thrown at it? And what would it actually take to make it stop?

## Part 2 — The Contract Behind Every Bill

Start with where this debt actually comes from.

In 1994, Pakistan had a different problem. Not too much debt, too little electricity.

Blackouts were common, factories couldn't get reliable power, and the government needed private companies to build power plants fast. [ANALYSIS: contextualizing the stated purpose of the 1994 Power Policy]

So it made those companies an offer. Build a power plant, and Pakistan will guarantee an 18 percent return, paid in a way that tracks the US dollar rather than the rupee. [SOURCE: SDPI, World Bank, via reporting] [VERIFY]

The International Monetary Fund and the Asian Development Bank helped design the mechanism that made that guarantee possible. [SOURCE: SDPI, World Bank, via reporting] [VERIFY]

It's called a capacity payment.

Here's what that actually means. Pakistan agreed to pay these power plants for being available to generate electricity, whether or not anyone actually used that electricity.

Think of it like a gym membership charged every month, whether you go three times a week or never show up. The plant gets paid either way.

That isn't a side effect of the system. It's the system, working exactly as designed.

By 2026, this fixed bill has become the single largest piece of what Pakistan pays for electricity. Regulators approved capacity charges of 17.19 rupees for every unit of power priced into the system for 2026. [SOURCE: NEPRA, The Nation, 2026] [VERIFY]

Out of every 100 rupees in Pakistan's total power purchase bill for that year, 63 go to capacity charges. Only 37 pay for the electricity actually burned and delivered. [SOURCE: NEPRA, 2026] [ANALYSIS: derived from NEPRA's reported total] [VERIFY]

Add it all up, and Pakistan paid 2.1 trillion rupees in capacity payments in 2024 alone, the number this episode's opening compared the whole debt to. [SOURCE: IEEFA, 2024] [VERIFY]

Here's the trap inside the trap. As Pakistan's industries used less power and demand on the grid shrank, the fixed capacity bill didn't shrink with it.

It just spread across a smaller amount of electricity actually sold, raising the price of every unit that did get used. [ANALYSIS] [SOURCE: IEEFA, 2024] [VERIFY]

Distribution companies are supposed to collect that price from every household and business in the country.

So how much of it do they actually collect?

## Part 3 — Who Doesn't Pay Their Share

Not all of it.

In the last financial year alone, Pakistan's distribution companies added 262 billion rupees to the debt through what regulators call inefficiency losses. [SOURCE: The News, 2026] [VERIFY]

That's electricity generated, sent down the wires, and never paid for. Some of it stolen, some of it simply never billed correctly.

That's a large number on its own. But it isn't even the biggest single non-payer.

That title belongs to K-Electric, the company that runs Karachi's power grid. In the same year, K-Electric added 194 billion rupees to the circular debt. [SOURCE: The News, 2026] [VERIFY]

By June 2026, its total unpaid bill had grown to 421 billion rupees, split between what it owes in principal and years of accumulated late fees. [SOURCE: The News, 2026] [VERIFY]

K-Electric says this isn't simply a case of refusing to pay. It says it's owed money too, over a regulatory dispute that goes back years.

In December 2020, Pakistan's power regulator approved a special pricing scheme for K-Electric, meant to reward the company for supplying extra electricity beyond a set baseline. [SOURCE: Business Recorder, via reporting] [VERIFY]

The government extended that scheme in 2021. But when the regulator later issued a new ruling on how to calculate it, K-Electric says the math changed in a way that broke the terms of its original, multi year tariff agreement. [SOURCE: Business Recorder, via reporting] [VERIFY]

The dispute is still unresolved. It has now reached Pakistan's Special Investment Facilitation Council, a body created specifically to unblock standoffs like this one. [SOURCE: Business Recorder, via reporting] [VERIFY]

Whoever turns out to be right, the practical effect is the same either way. Hundreds of billions of rupees sit frozen in a dispute, unpaid, while the debt they're part of keeps compounding. [ANALYSIS]

Distribution losses and K-Electric's dispute together explain a large share of why Pakistan's power sector can't pay its own bills on time.

But the power sector doesn't only owe money to itself. It owes money to the company that supplies its fuel.

## Part 4 — The Debt That Jumps Sectors

Here's where the power sector's unpaid bills stop being just a power sector problem.

Many of Pakistan's power plants don't burn coal or diesel. They burn natural gas, bought from state gas utilities like SNGPL.

When the government's own power-purchasing agency doesn't pay those plants on time, the plants can't pay their gas bills either. [ANALYSIS]

This isn't a theory. There's a documented case that shows exactly how it happens.

Liberty Daharki Power Limited runs a gas fired power plant. By December 2025, it owed SNGPL just over 20 billion rupees in unpaid gas bills. [SOURCE: Profit by Pakistan Today, Business Recorder, 2025] [VERIFY]

Its security deposit, the cash SNGPL holds specifically to cover exactly this kind of shortfall, was only 3.085 billion rupees. The debt was more than six times the safety net meant to catch it. [SOURCE: Profit by Pakistan Today, 2025] [ANALYSIS: derived ratio] [VERIFY]

SNGPL warned it would cut the plant's gas supply. Liberty Daharki's response was blunt.

It said it couldn't pay because it hadn't been paid, blaming limited payments from CPPA-G, the government body that buys electricity from every power plant in the country. [SOURCE: Business Recorder, 2025] [VERIFY]

One power plant, one gas utility, one missed government payment. That's the entire mechanism, in miniature.

Zoom out, and the same pattern repeats at national scale. SNGPL's own unpaid bills reached 819 billion rupees by June 2026, close to a quarter of the entire gas sector's total debt. [SOURCE: Express Tribune, Profit by Pakistan Today, 2026] [ANALYSIS: derived share] [VERIFY]

Government officials have confirmed the link directly. Between April and June 2026, gas meant for power plants was priced at 2,000 rupees per unit, far below the 3,498 to 4,375 rupees the fuel actually cost to import.

The Petroleum Division itself warned this pricing gap would create a revenue shortfall for SNGPL and make the gas sector's debt harder to reduce. [SOURCE: EnergyUpdate.com.pk, 2026]

So is the power sector the real cause of the gas sector's debt? Not mainly, and the honest number says so.

SNGPL's specific receivables from the power sector ran somewhere between 76.5 billion and 148.5 billion rupees across 2025, real money, but a small slice of its 819 billion rupee total. [SOURCE: multiple outlets, via reporting] [ESTIMATE: roughly 9 to 14 percent of the total] [VERIFY]

Most of that total traces back to something else entirely. Gas prices that haven't kept up with what the fuel actually costs, a gap the gas sector has been absorbing since 2013. [SOURCE: Express Tribune, 2026] [VERIFY]

The power sector's unpaid bills don't cause the gas sector's debt. They make an already large problem worse, in a way that's easy to miss, because the money moves sideways between two sectors that report their books separately. [ANALYSIS]

So if the contract is the root, and non-payment and gas exposure are the amplifiers, has anyone actually tried to fix this? And did it work?

## Part 5 — The Reforms That Keep Missing

Pakistan has tried to fix this before. Four times, in fact.

Governments renegotiated power contracts with private producers in 1998, in 2012, in 2020, and again in 2024. [SOURCE: Dawn, via reporting] [VERIFY]

The most recent round was the most aggressive yet. Five power producers had their contracts terminated outright. [SOURCE: multiple outlets, via reporting] [VERIFY]

Two of them accepted discounted settlements worth up to 20 billion rupees. Eighteen more agreed to convert from the old take-or-pay arrangement to a new take-and-pay one, meaning the government would only pay for electricity it actually used. [SOURCE: multiple outlets, via reporting] [VERIFY]

The government's own Power Division put a number on what all of this would save across 29 renegotiated contracts: 3.498 trillion rupees. [SOURCE: EnergyUpdate.com.pk, 2025] [VERIFY]

That's a genuinely large number. It's also, on its own, not proof of anything.

Here's the test. If the fix is real, the debt should stop growing, or at least grow more slowly.

In the same year all of this was being announced, Pakistan's power sector still added 364 billion rupees in new circular debt, the exact figure this episode opened with. [SOURCE: Express Tribune, 2026]

Government subsidy injections brought that number down to a net increase of 61 billion rupees. Still growing, and only because the government spent 302 billion rupees in fresh subsidies to make it look smaller. [SOURCE: The News, 2026]

The International Monetary Fund had asked Pakistan to keep the year's new debt under roughly 300 to 325 billion rupees. Even with the subsidy, Pakistan didn't hit that number either. [SOURCE: multiple outlets, via reporting] [VERIFY]

So what does that tell us. Four renegotiation rounds, trillions of rupees in claimed savings, and the debt is still growing faster than the country's own lender wanted it to.

That's not because the renegotiations were fake. Some of those savings are likely real.

It's because every round has worked on the edges of the contracts, adjusting individual tariffs, converting individual plants, terminating individual deals, without changing the take-or-pay structure that created the problem in 1994. [ANALYSIS]

A real fix would mean something more specific than another renegotiation.

Distribution companies that actually collect what they're owed, even when enforcing that is unpopular.

A resolution to K-Electric's dispute, one way or the other, instead of another year frozen in arbitration.

Gas and power sector accounts settled together, not separately, so a shortfall in one stops quietly becoming a debt in the other.

And new contracts, not just renegotiated old ones, built without a guarantee that gets paid whether the power is used or not.

None of that has happened yet.

Until it does, the number this episode opened with will keep doing what it's done for three decades. Growing, no matter how many billions get spent trying to make it stop.
