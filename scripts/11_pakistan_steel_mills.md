## Editorial frame

### Approved angle

PSM: The SOE That Ate Itself — how a series of decisions, each rational for the actor making it, collectively produced an institution that can neither function nor be dissolved.

### Central question

How did Pakistan's only integrated steel mill become a facility that costs billions without producing anything?

### Thesis

PSM did not fail because of a single corrupt leadership or one bad decision. It failed because a sequence of individually defensible choices — by governments, courts, unions, and lenders — removed every mechanism that could have forced a correction.

### Story logic

Institutional and decision-focused. The story follows a chain of institutional decisions, each traceable to a specific actor under a specific constraint, that together produced an impossible operating position.

### Structure rationale

Four parts. Part 1 establishes the current anomaly: a mill that costs without producing. Part 2 explains why the mill was built and why the logic held at the time. Part 3 traces the chain of decisions that destroyed its commercial viability. Part 4 quantifies what Pakistan pays for the unresolved status and what the realistic paths forward look like. A fifth part would split what Part 3 covers without adding analytical content.

## Part 1 — The Factory That Costs Without Producing

There is a steel mill on the outskirts of Karachi, at a place called Bin Qasim, that has not produced a tonne of steel in nearly a decade.

The mill covers thousands of acres. It has blast furnaces, coke ovens, a rolling plant, a power station, and a port facility. The infrastructure required to make steel is largely still there. What it no longer has is steel production.

Pakistan Steel Mills, which the government built through the 1970s and 1980s, stopped producing in 2015. [VERIFY exact shutdown date — some sources cite mid-2015, others late-2015] Since then, it has drawn on government support to cover payroll, maintenance, and debt servicing, while generating no revenue from the activity it was built to perform.

The accumulated losses reported against PSM's books are substantial. Parliamentary committee filings and government disclosures have cited figures exceeding Rs 200 billion. [VERIFY — current figure may be significantly higher; sources differ on accounting treatment and whether contingent liabilities are included] The workforce, which at its peak exceeded 12,000 employees, stood at roughly 9,000 when the mill stopped functioning. Most of those employees remain on the payroll today. [VERIFY current headcount — Ministry of Industries testimony is the primary source]

The question this episode investigates is not simply why PSM failed. State enterprises fail for many reasons. The more specific question is why Pakistan has been unable to either restart it or resolve it — why the same decision has been deferred across successive governments, each of which inherited the problem and passed it on unchanged.

How does a national industrial project become an institution that costs billions and produces nothing? And why, after a decade of inaction, has it remained impossible to make a decision in either direction?

## Part 2 — Why It Was Built

In 1971, Pakistan lost half its territory. The country that emerged from that war faced a legitimacy crisis, an economic contraction, and pressure to demonstrate that the remaining state could build something.

Zulfikar Ali Bhutto came to power in December 1971 with a mandate to restructure the economy. His government moved to nationalise a wide range of industries and launched several large state enterprises. Pakistan Steel Mills was among the most significant of these projects. [SOURCE: Dawn, historical reporting on Bhutto-era industrialisation policy]

The development logic of the era supported this kind of investment. Import substitution industrialisation — the theory that developing countries should build domestic production capacity for essential goods rather than import them — was the mainstream economic policy framework across South Asia and much of the developing world through the 1960s and 1970s. Governments in India, Egypt, Turkey, and Brazil were all building state steel mills on the same premise.

For Pakistan specifically, the economic case was concrete. Steel was a key input for construction, railways, manufacturing, and infrastructure. Pakistan imported all of it, paying in foreign exchange. A domestic mill would reduce that import bill, provide a local supply base for downstream industries, and create skilled industrial employment. This was not a speculative argument.

The Soviet Union provided both financing and technical assistance to build PSM. The USSR had done the same for India's Bhilai and Bokaro steel plants, and similar arrangements existed with other developing countries. The Bin Qasim location was chosen for practical reasons: close to Karachi's port, where the iron ore and coking coal PSM would need to import could be unloaded and transported directly to the plant. [SOURCE: PSM historical documentation; ANALYSIS — logistical rationale is consistent with publicly available site descriptions]

PSM was designed for an initial production capacity of 1.1 million tonnes per annum, with provisions for later expansion. [VERIFY designed capacity] Construction began in the early 1970s and the first steel came out of the mill in the early 1980s. [VERIFY production start — commonly cited as 1981–82]

The original decision, assessed against the information available at the time, was not unreasonable. The error was not in the decision to build a steel mill. The error accumulated over the four decades that followed.

## Part 3 — The Decisions That Broke It

The first structural problem was built into PSM's commercial design from the beginning.

A state enterprise insulated from competition does not face the same consequence for inefficiency as a private company does. A private steel producer that loses customers to cheaper imports eventually has to cut costs, restructure, or close. A state enterprise in the same position can draw on government support and continue without changing anything. This difference shapes every decision that follows.

PSM operated under administered pricing — the government determined what PSM could charge, not the market. For most of its early operating life, PSM had no direct domestic competitor for primary steel. This removed the commercial pressure that would have forced efficiency improvements or investment in modernisation. The mill could run at a loss as long as the government was prepared to cover it.

Through the 1980s and 1990s, the global steel market changed in ways that made PSM's position progressively harder to defend. International steel prices fell as excess production from Eastern European countries, the former Soviet states, and eventually China entered global markets. Cheaper imported steel became available. And within Pakistan, a domestic re-rolling industry developed — private mills that used scrap steel as their input rather than iron ore and coke. Their capital cost was lower, their workforce was smaller, their legacy debt was zero. They could produce construction steel, the most commercially important category, at a cost PSM could not match. [REPORTED: Business Recorder, multiple reports from the late 1990s and 2000s on PSM cost competitiveness]

PSM's workforce grew through mechanisms that had nothing to do with production requirements. Each government found it easier to add employees, or to avoid removing them, than to restructure. The workforce that had built political relationships, organised effectively, and expected continued employment made any restructuring attempt a direct political confrontation. None of the relevant governments was prepared to have that confrontation.

The privatisation attempt of 2006 is the clearest single demonstration of how these pressures worked. [VERIFY — some accounts place the key Supreme Court ruling in 2006, others in 2007] The government initiated a process to sell a majority stake in PSM to a private investor. The transaction was challenged in the courts and the Supreme Court ruled that the privatisation process had not met procedural requirements under Pakistani law. The sale was blocked.

The legal challenge was real. But the underlying problem was not procedural. Any buyer willing to pay a meaningful price for PSM's assets would need to restructure the workforce. A mill that employed tens of thousands of people while producing steel at a fraction of its designed capacity was not a commercially viable operating base. [VERIFY workforce and capacity utilisation at the time of the attempted sale] Restructuring required severance payments, legal exposure, and political capital. It also required a buyer prepared to absorb those costs and still make the investment viable on the other side. No such buyer appeared at the asking price the government required.

The privatisation failed. PSM continued. The workforce remained. The debt continued to accumulate.

After 2006, the available options narrowed with each passing year. Restarting PSM at a competitive cost required modernising the plant — replacing equipment that had not been maintained or upgraded to the standard needed for efficient large-scale steelmaking. That modernisation required investment. But the government was unwilling to commit capital to a mill with an uncertain commercial case, and private investors would not invest without first resolving the workforce. The workforce resolution required political will no government was prepared to sustain.

By 2015, PSM's cost of producing one tonne of steel exceeded the price it could charge for that steel in the Pakistani market. [VERIFY this unit-economics figure — reported in Pakistani press but the exact margin is not clearly documented in publicly available filings] The mill reduced output progressively and eventually stopped. The workers stayed. The debt stayed. The question of what to do with PSM moved to the next government, which moved it to the next.

This is the mechanics of institutional failure as distinct from individual corruption. Each actor made a decision that was defensible from their own position. The government avoided a political confrontation it could not win. The unions protected their members' employment against a restructuring that offered them nothing. The courts enforced procedural requirements that existed for legitimate reasons. The lenders extended credit against government guarantees because the alternative — forcing a default — created systemic risks. Together, these individually rational decisions produced a position from which no rational individual actor had an incentive to exit.

The mill did not collapse. It suspended. And suspension turned out to be more expensive than any available decision.

## Part 4 — What Pakistan Pays

Pakistan's construction sector, infrastructure projects, and manufacturing base buy steel. They buy it from importers, because there is no functional domestic producer of primary steel.

Pakistan imports between four and seven million tonnes of steel products annually, depending on construction activity and public infrastructure spending. [VERIFY — figure fluctuates significantly year to year; cite most recent SBP or PBS trade data] The import bill for steel and steel products is consistently one of the larger items in Pakistan's import account, settled in US dollars at the global market price.

A functioning domestic steel producer would not have eliminated that import dependence. Pakistan's steel consumption, in a normal construction year, exceeds what PSM at full capacity was ever designed to produce. But a working mill producing around one million tonnes would displace a portion of imports, give buyers a domestic alternative, and establish a pricing benchmark that disciplines what importers can charge.

Without that benchmark, Pakistani steel buyers are fully exposed to the commodity cycle. When global steel prices rise — driven by Chinese output decisions, freight costs, or coking coal supply constraints — every construction project in Pakistan absorbs the premium with no domestic alternative to turn to. That premium moves through into housing costs, infrastructure budgets, and the cost base of every firm using steel as a production input.

The construction industry has adapted. It buys from whatever importer offers the best available price. It cannot choose a domestic mill that does not function. The result is a steel market where Pakistan is a price-taker — a buyer with no domestic production position to fall back on in a tight market.

On the other side of the ledger, PSM's costs continue. The government finances the payroll of employees who are not producing. It services the debt that financed a mill that is not operating. It pays maintenance costs on equipment that is deteriorating. The exact annual carrying cost of keeping PSM in its current state — not operating, not resolved — is not consolidated into a single publicly available figure. [VERIFY — Ministry of Industries and Finance Division disclosures, and parliamentary budget submissions, are the primary sources]

Pakistan is therefore paying twice simultaneously. It pays the import cost for steel it cannot produce domestically. And it pays the carrying cost of the facility that was supposed to produce it.

The realistic paths forward have not changed in substance since 2006. Privatisation requires either a buyer prepared to accept the workforce obligations or a government willing to fund workforce restructuring before the sale — reducing the liability to the point where a buyer's commercial case works. Closure requires the same workforce settlement plus a negotiated resolution of the outstanding debt. A government-funded restart requires a credible operating plan, modernised plant, a workforce scaled to production rather than politics, and a commercial market into which PSM can sell competitively.

None of these paths is simple. Each requires a specific constituency to absorb a real cost: workers accepting redundancy, lenders accepting restructured terms, or taxpayers funding modernisation with uncertain returns. The political economy of each path involves distributing losses that currently sit off balance sheets and outside immediate view.

What is clear is the cost of the fourth option — continuing as now. Each year of inaction compounds the accumulated losses, further deteriorates the plant, and extends Pakistan's dependency on imported steel. The financial case for a decision has been visible for more than a decade. The political case for making one has so far proved harder to resolve than the financial arithmetic.

The mill at Bin Qasim was built to reduce Pakistan's dependence on imported steel. After fifty years, Pakistan imports more steel than it ever has. The mill has not produced any of it.
