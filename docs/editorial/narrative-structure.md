# Structure and Narrative Design

Load this file when proposing or approving a script structure.
It supplements `CLAUDE.md`, which takes precedence on any conflict.

---

## Core principle

Every long-form episode must have an intentional narrative structure, but no fixed
section count or universal middle-section template applies.

The structure must be selected after the angle is approved and before full drafting
begins.

---

## Story logic types

The structure may follow one of these narrative logics, or a justified combination:

- **Causal** — one event or decision produces another.
- **Chronological** — events unfold over time in a meaningful sequence.
- **Financial** — cost and revenue mechanics drive the central argument.
- **Institutional** — how an organisation makes decisions and absorbs incentives.
- **Comparative** — two options, systems, or countries measured against each other.
- **Operational** — how something actually works day to day vs. how it is described.
- **Regulatory** — how rules create or distort incentives.
- **Company-focused** — one company's decisions and their consequences.
- **Decision-focused** — one or several key decisions that changed the outcome.
- **Structural or historical** — how a long-cycle pattern developed and persists.

---

## Narrative functions that never flex

Regardless of section count or logic type, every episode must contain:

1. A concrete entry point.
2. A clear central question.
3. A logical development from evidence to analysis.
4. Evidence before major conclusions.
5. A grounded ending.

These are narrative functions, not fixed section names or counts.

---

## Opening function

The opening must accomplish three things:

1. Establish a concrete documented anomaly or consequence.
2. Give the viewer a reason to keep watching.
3. State the central question the episode will answer.

Use sentence-length variation naturally. Do not force a pattern of fragments or
repeated emphatic declarations. Do not use formulaic closing phrases such as
"That was not an accident." For full opening style guidance, see
`docs/editorial/storytelling.md` — Opening rhythm.

---

## Closing function

The closing must accomplish three things:

1. Name the most defensible finding the evidence supports.
2. Identify what would need to change and what the realistic obstacles are.
3. Say who this matters to and why.

Do not repeat the thesis in different words. End with a practical implication.

---

## Mandatory pre-writing stages

Complete these stages in order. Do not begin full script drafting until Stage 3
is approved.

### Stage 1 — Story design

Produce a story design document using the template at `docs/templates/story-design.md`.

Do not begin full script drafting until the story design shows a clear causal,
institutional, or chronological progression, and all major positions in the
narrative chain have evidence assigned to them.

### Stage 2 — Fact-to-story test

For every major fact, identify its function. A fact must do at least one of:

- Establish the baseline.
- Show a change.
- Prove a mechanism.
- Reveal an incentive.
- Introduce a conflict.
- Demonstrate a consequence.
- Challenge an assumption.
- Support or weaken the thesis.
- Establish uncertainty.
- Set up the next part.

If a fact has no clear function, remove it. Do not include a number merely because
it is available. Do not include a source merely because it is reputable. Every
statistic must change the viewer's understanding or move the argument forward.

### Stage 3 — Structure approval

Propose and document:

- The number of parts.
- The name and distinct purpose of each part.
- Why this structure fits the approved angle and central question.
- Which evidence anchors each section.
- Where evidence is thin or a `[VERIFY]` item is load-bearing.

Do not begin writing until the structure is explicitly approved.

## Legacy templates for middle sections

The three templates below define common section names for the middle parts of a
five-section episode. They remain valid when the story logic fits them cleanly.
Template choice follows the central question, not the subject category:

- "How does this cost or earn money?" — Template A or B.
- "Why does this exist / how did we get here?" — Template C.

These are defaults, not requirements. A five-section format should only be used
when the story logic genuinely calls for it or the user explicitly requests it.

---

**Template A — Macro / Institutions**
*Use for: government policy, SOEs, energy, monetary and fiscal topics, regulatory
structures, trade policy, sector-wide failures.*

| Section | Name | Focus |
|---------|------|-------|
| 2 | The Paper Trail | PKR unit economics, government budgets, subsidy flows, cost-margin anatomy in rupees |
| 3 | The Field Reality | How the system runs day to day; informal networks, the cash layer, middleman dynamics the numbers alone don't show |
| 4 | The Systemic Domino Effect | How this one system ripples into the wider economy; SBP / FBR / FX / energy connections |

---

**Template B — Company Case Study**
*Use for: named companies, corporate strategy, industry deep-dives, listed or
private firms, sector leaders and failures.*

| Section | Name | Focus |
|---------|------|-------|
| 2 | The Business Model | Revenue model, unit economics, margin structure — how this company actually makes or loses money |
| 3 | The Operational Reality | Supply chain, workforce, compliance, the informal layer, what actually makes the operation run |
| 4 | The Competitive Position | Market position vs. regional and global peers; FX, energy, and regulatory exposure; what threatens the model |

---

**Template C — Structural Dependency / History**
*Use for: long-cycle structural stories (remittances, migration, debt cycles, land),
historical investigations ("how did we get here"), and policy decisions whose
consequences play out over decades.*

| Section | Name | Focus |
|---------|------|-------|
| 2 | The Origin | How and when the structure was built; the decision, policy, or event that created it |
| 3 | How It Plays Out | Ground-level consequences — households, towns, industries — that the origin story produced |
| 4 | The Structural Risk | What threatens the model, what has already begun to change, and what the numbers say |
