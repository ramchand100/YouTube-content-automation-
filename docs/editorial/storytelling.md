# Documentary Storytelling and Tone

Load this file when writing or reviewing scripts.
It supplements `CLAUDE.md`, which takes precedence on any conflict.

---

## Examples in this file

Examples below are illustrative, not templates.

Do not copy the rhythm, sentence count, emotional temperature, or wording of an
example unless the topic genuinely requires it. The writer must generate a fresh
opening appropriate to the documented event and approved angle.

---

## Core principle

The script must be an evidence-based story, not a research summary.

Facts are the evidence. The story is the sequence of decisions, incentives,
failures, adaptations, and consequences that those facts reveal.

---

## Central narrative principle

Every episode must answer four questions:

1. What changed?
2. Why did it change?
3. Who adapted or benefited?
4. Who carries the cost now?

The script must make the connection between these questions visible. Do not
simply place facts next to one another.

---

## Story movement

Every major part of the script must do at least one of the following:

- Introduce a concrete problem.
- Challenge an obvious explanation.
- Reveal a hidden mechanism.
- Show a decision or incentive.
- Demonstrate a consequence.
- Introduce a counterargument.
- Narrow or revise the thesis.
- Change the viewer's understanding of the problem.
- Establish what would need to change.

A paragraph that only adds information without moving the argument should be
removed or combined with another paragraph.

---

## Documentary spine

Before writing, establish this internal chain:

```
Concrete anomaly
→ viewer assumption
→ evidence that complicates the assumption
→ mechanism
→ decision or incentive
→ consequence
→ wider economic effect
→ realistic answer
```

The final script does not need to display these labels, but the narration must
follow a clear version of this movement.

---

## Concrete entry point

Open with a documented event, decision, transaction, failure, price, shutdown,
filing, policy change, or institutional action.

Do not open with a general definition, a list of historical facts, or broad
statements such as:

- "Pakistan has always struggled with..."
- "In today's rapidly changing world..."
- "The economy faces many challenges."
- "This is an important issue."
- "The numbers tell a troubling story."

If a personal scene is not documented, use an institutional or commercial scene:
a port waiting for cargo, a factory choosing between transport options, a company
filing showing a cost shift, a ministry changing an allocation, a regulator
changing a tariff, a railway losing a major freight contract.

Do not invent people, dialogue, private thoughts, or physical scenes.

---

## Opening rhythm

The opening must be clear, specific, and immediately understandable.

Begin with the strongest documented anomaly or consequence. This may be a real
decision, transaction, filing, price, shutdown, failed project, lost customer, or
measurable change.

Use sentence-length variation naturally. Short sentences may create emphasis, but
do not force a sequence of fragments or repeated declarations.

Do not use formulaic lines such as:

- "That was not an accident."
- "That was not a coincidence."
- "That gap did not appear by itself."
- "The numbers tell the story."
- "But the real story is."

If the evidence suggests that the outcome came from a chain of decisions, policies,
or incentives, explain that chain directly. Do not imply deliberate intent unless
the evidence establishes intent.

End the opening by stating the central question in a natural sentence. The opening
must create curiosity through an unresolved mechanism or contradiction, not through
manufactured suspense.

---

## Narrative tension

Create tension through competing realities, not hype. Useful tensions include:

- The cheaper option is less reliable.
- The profitable service carries a public obligation.
- The policy helps one group while raising costs for another.
- A reform improves the balance sheet but harms a stakeholder.
- A company grows revenue while losing cash.
- A sector expands while margins shrink.
- A solution exists technically but is blocked institutionally.
- The market appears inefficient until the hidden constraint is revealed.

Do not manufacture suspense. Do not use exaggerated words to create importance.
Let the conflict between incentives and outcomes carry the tension.

---

## Incentive-based tension

Whenever the story involves institutional failure, identify the competing
incentives. For each major actor, ask:

- What does this actor want?
- What constraint does this actor face?
- What decision follows?
- Who benefits from that decision?
- Who bears its cost?
- Is the outcome intentional, tolerated, or unintended?

The story should show how individually rational decisions can produce a damaging
system-wide result. Do not reduce complex outcomes to villains or heroes.

Example chain:

```
The ministry wants to keep fares affordable.
The railway needs higher revenue.
The passenger wants a cheap ticket.
The taxpayer covers the gap.
The freight division loses investment.
```

That is a story. It is not merely a list of facts.

---

## Concrete explanation

Do not describe a problem only as a category.

**Weak:**
> The company faced regulatory pressure.

**Stronger:**
> The regulator capped the price the company could charge, but did not remove the
> cost of imported fuel. The company therefore sold more units while earning less
> on each one.

Whenever possible, show:

1. The condition.
2. The action it causes.
3. The immediate result.
4. The person, business, or institution that absorbs the effect.

Prefer visible actions over abstract labels.

---

## Documentary scene logic

A documentary scene does not require invented characters or dialogue.

A scene may be:

- A company making a documented decision.
- A factory changing suppliers.
- A port handling a cargo backlog.
- A government changing a tariff.
- A bank changing its lending policy.
- A listed company reporting a margin decline.
- A railway losing a freight contract.
- A household or business facing a documented price increase.

When a documented scene is unavailable, narrate the mechanism directly.

Do not invent sensory detail, dialogue, private motives, or unnamed personal
stories to make the script feel cinematic.

Use this sequence where evidence allows:

- What happened?
- What decision was made?
- Why did it make commercial sense at the time?
- What did it change?
- Who benefited?
- What cost appeared elsewhere?

---

## Viewer journey

At the start, the viewer should have a reasonable but incomplete explanation.

During the episode, the evidence should make that explanation insufficient.

The script should then reveal the mechanism that explains the contradiction.

By the end, the viewer should understand:

- What changed.
- Why the obvious explanation is incomplete.
- Which incentives produced the outcome.
- Where the cost or benefit appears.
- What can realistically change.

Do not reveal the final interpretation before the evidence has earned it.
Do not hide the thesis until the final paragraph.
Give the viewer enough direction to understand what the investigation is testing.

---

## Facts inside arguments

Do not present statistics as isolated objects.

**Weak:**
> Pakistan Railways earned Rs 88.8 billion. Freight contributed Rs 28 billion.
> Passenger services contributed Rs 47 billion.

**Stronger:**
> Pakistan Railways earned Rs 88.8 billion in FY2023-24, but freight contributed
> only Rs 28 billion. Passenger services contributed Rs 47 billion. The revenue
> mix shows what the railway had become: a passenger service carrying a shrinking
> freight business.

The stronger version does not merely report numbers. It tells the viewer what
the numbers mean.

---

## No fact-only paragraphs

A paragraph must not exist only to list dates, revenue figures, policy changes,
company names, market shares, historical events, or institutional descriptions.

If a paragraph contains factual information, it must also explain at least one of:

- Why the fact matters.
- What changed because of it.
- Which incentive it reveals.
- Which assumption it challenges.
- Who carries the consequence.
- How it sets up the next question.

Use this paragraph pattern:

```
Fact or event
→ meaning
→ mechanism or consequence
→ next question
```

A fact-only paragraph belongs in the research brief, not the final script.

---

## Evidence-to-meaning rule

After every important statistic, event, or quotation, write one sentence explaining
what it proves, suggests, or fails to prove.

Use calibrated language:

- "This shows..."
- "This suggests..."
- "The important point is..."
- "That comparison matters because..."
- "This does not prove..., but it does show..."
- "The figure is significant because..."

Do not use interpretation merely as decoration. It must explain the mechanism or
change the argument.

---

## Human consequence without fictional characters

Human relevance does not require invented characters. Show consequences through:

- Factory costs.
- Delivery delays.
- Food prices.
- Export margins.
- Household bills.
- Employment.
- Working capital.
- Inventory.
- Tax exposure.
- Investment decisions.
- Regional access.
- Business closures.
- Public-service tradeoffs.

Use a real company, location, or industry only when the source supports the claim.

---

## Institutions as actors

Do not describe institutions as abstract nouns only. Explain what each
institution did or failed to do.

**Weak:** Infrastructure prioritisation created an imbalance.

**Stronger:** Successive budgets expanded road capacity while railway investment
remained too limited to modernise the freight network.

The second version gives the viewer an action, a comparison, and a consequence.

---

## Explain the system through decisions

Whenever possible, connect a structural result to a decision:

- Who made the decision?
- What problem were they solving?
- What incentive shaped the choice?
- What did the decision improve?
- What cost did it create?
- Who absorbed that cost?
- Was the outcome intended or unintended?

Do not assign motives without evidence. Use "the effect was," "the incentive
was," or "the policy created" when intent cannot be proven. Describe the
decision and its effect in administrative or economic terms, not as political
strategy — see `docs/editorial/prose-style.md`, "Political neutrality," for
worked examples.

---

## Section and part transitions

Do not use headings as the only structure. End each part by creating the next
analytical question. Examples:

- "If roads were more flexible, why did rail lose even bulk cargo?"
- "That explains the customer's decision. It does not explain why the railway
  could not replace the lost business."
- "The revenue problem is visible in the accounts. The operating problem appears
  on the ground."
- "That policy solved an immediate bottleneck. It also changed who controlled
  the freight market."
- "The cost did not disappear when cargo left rail. It moved into the road system."

A transition must move the investigation forward.

---

## The conclusion

Do not repeat the thesis in different words. The ending must answer:

- What is the most defensible finding?
- What remains uncertain?
- What would need to change?
- What cannot be fixed quickly?
- Who should care?
- What decision follows for founders, companies, investors, professionals, or
  students?

End with a practical implication, not a motivational slogan.

---

## Final documentary test

Before delivering the script, identify the following for every major part:

- The opening problem.
- The viewer's likely assumption.
- The new evidence.
- The mechanism revealed.
- The incentive conflict.
- The consequence.
- The next question.

If any part cannot be identified, the writing is probably descriptive rather than
investigative.

Then remove or rewrite:

- Any paragraph that only lists facts.
- Any paragraph that repeats an earlier explanation.
- Any statistic without a clear interpretation.
- Any conclusion that appears before its supporting evidence.
- Any sentence whose only purpose is to sound dramatic.
- Any transition that does not create a genuine next question.
