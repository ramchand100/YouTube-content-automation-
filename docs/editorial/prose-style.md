# Prose Style and Language Rules

Load this file when writing or reviewing scripts.
It supplements `CLAUDE.md`, which takes precedence on any conflict.

---

## Channel Register

The channel's emotional register is:

- Authoritative, but not preachy.
- Analytical, but not cold.
- Engaged, but not outraged.
- Serious, but not gloomy.
- Skeptical, but not cynical.
- Local, but not parochial.
- Accessible, but not simplistic.
- Confident, but not absolute.

The narrator sounds like a well-informed business journalist explaining how a system
actually works to a smart colleague.

The narrator is interested in consequences, incentives, and mechanics.

The narrator does not perform anger, surprise, patriotism, disgust, or optimism.

### Emotional distance

Maintain professional emotional distance from the subject.

Do not tell the viewer what to feel.

Instead of:
> This shocking failure destroyed thousands of businesses.

Use:
> The policy reduced the company's cash flow, and several suppliers carried the loss
> through delayed payments and smaller orders.

Instead of:
> The government completely ignored the problem.

Use:
> The policy addressed the immediate shortage but did not remove the supply constraint.

Instead of:
> This is a disaster waiting to happen.

Use:
> The current arrangement leaves the system exposed if demand rises or external
> financing becomes less available.

### Authority

Sound authoritative through:

- Accurate facts.
- Clear explanations.
- Specific comparisons.
- Transparent uncertainty.
- Controlled language.
- Strong causal reasoning.

Do not sound authoritative through:

- Grand declarations.
- Moral certainty.
- Aggressive adjectives.
- Excessive confidence.
- Repeated claims that the issue is obvious.

## Language

Use strictly professional, direct, engaging English.

Do not use Urdu, Roman Urdu, or Urdu-English hybrid language in voiceover or
on-screen script text.

Local terms with no clean English equivalent may be used only as named analytical
concepts. Define each term in plain English the first time it appears.

Write for an intelligent viewer who knows little about finance or economics.

Use simple words without simplifying the analysis.

Prefer:

- Concrete nouns.
- Active verbs.
- Short, natural sentences.
- Specific examples.
- Visible consequences.
- Plain explanations of technical mechanics.

Avoid:

- Academic language.
- Bureaucratic language.
- Corporate jargon.
- Abstract nouns stacked together.
- Long sentences with several clauses.
- Passive voice where active voice is clearer.
- Statistics without interpretation.
- Conclusions that appear before the evidence.

---

## Spoken-language standard

The script must sound natural when read aloud by a professional narrator.

It must not sound like:

- A report being read aloud.
- A university lecture.
- A policy memo.
- A newspaper editorial.
- A list of statistics.
- An AI-generated explainer.

Read every paragraph as spoken audio.

If a paragraph sounds like written research rather than spoken analysis, rewrite it.

---

## Sentence rhythm

Do not make every sentence short.

Use varied rhythm:

- Short sentences for weight.
- Medium sentences for explanation.
- Longer sentences only when connecting a clear chain of cause and effect.

A normal paragraph should not contain a series of dramatic fragments.

Use fragments rarely and deliberately.

Do not repeat the same sentence pattern across paragraphs.

---

## Analytical clarity

When a technical term is necessary:

1. Name it.
2. Define it in plain English.
3. Show how it works in Pakistan.
4. Explain who pays, gains, or carries the risk.

Do not define a term and then immediately move to another unrelated fact.

Keep the viewer inside the mechanism.

---

## Em dashes

Avoid em dashes where a comma or period will do.

Use em dashes only when setting off a genuinely parenthetical phrase that would be
ambiguous with commas.

---

## Pacing and information density

Do not place several statistics in consecutive sentences unless they form one clear
comparison.

After a major figure, explain its meaning before introducing another figure.

Alternate between:

- Concrete situation.
- Explanation.
- Number.
- Interpretation.
- Consequence.
- Transition.

Do not use more than three consecutive sentences that only report facts.

Do not use more than two consecutive paragraphs without a concrete consequence,
decision, conflict, or example.

Every 45 to 90 seconds of narration should contain at least one of:

- A new discovery.
- A changed assumption.
- A concrete consequence.
- A meaningful comparison.
- A decision point.
- A counterargument.
- A shift in scale from company to sector, or sector to household.

---

## Research notes are not narration

Research briefs may contain source disputes, alternative figures, caveats,
methodological notes, long explanations, unresolved claims, and questions for
further research.

The final script must convert those notes into clean narration.

Do not read research notes aloud. Do not place these phrases in final narration:

- "Primary document not directly accessed."
- "Date of claim unconfirmed."
- "Figure attributed to media reports."
- "Source may reflect an older period."
- "Treat as a working estimate until confirmed."

Use `[VERIFY]` in the script where necessary, and place the detailed explanation
in the source audit file.

---

## Claim classification

Add a classification label before or after any claim where ambiguity exists:

- **VERIFIED** — supported by a named primary source.
- **REPORTED** — credible journalism, not independently confirmed; attribute it.
- **ANALYSIS** — interpretation drawn from verified evidence; label it.
- **ESTIMATE** — calculation with method shown.
- **UNRESOLVED** — conflicting sources; tag `[VERIFY]` and note the conflict.

Never present ANALYSIS as fact. Never present ESTIMATE as a VERIFIED figure.

## Numbers, Units, and Currency

### Currency standard

Use `Rs` for Pakistani rupees in narration and on-screen text.

Do not alternate between Rs., PKR, rupees, Pakistani rupees.

Use `Rs` consistently, except where a source's exact notation must be reproduced.

Examples:
- Rs 5 million.
- Rs 2.4 billion.
- Rs 88.8 billion.
- Rs 450 per unit.

Use `PKR` only when comparing currencies in a table, or when the source uses a
currency-code format that must be preserved.

### International number system

Use million, billion, and trillion in voiceover.

Do not use lakh, crore, arab, or kharab in narration unless the local term is
essential to the source or audience context.

If a local unit materially matters, define it once:

> The government allocated Rs 10 billion, or 1,000 crore in the local numbering system.

After that, use billion or million consistently. One billion equals 100 crore.

### Rounding

Round to the level the argument requires.

Use:
- Rs 2.4 billion, not Rs 2,437,829,442.
- 8.2 million tonnes, not 8,197,341 tonnes.
- 17.5 percent, where the decimal changes the interpretation.
- 18 percent, where it does not.

Never imply precision the source does not support.

### Spoken numbers

Write numbers in the form a narrator would naturally say.

Use: Rs 2.4 billion. Not: Pakistani rupees 2.4 billion.

Use: 17 percent. Not: 17 percentage points — unless the figure specifically measures
a percentage-point change.

### Percentages and percentage points

Use "percent" for a share or rate.

Use "percentage points" for the difference between two percentages.

Example:
> The tax rate rose from 15 percent to 20 percent, an increase of five percentage points.

### Foreign-currency conversions

Use the source currency when the transaction, debt, or investment is denominated
in that currency.

Convert to rupees only when the conversion helps a Pakistani viewer understand the
scale. If using a conversion:

- State the exchange-rate date or period.
- Use an approximate conversion.
- Do not mix a historical amount with a current exchange rate without saying so.
- Use one currency consistently for the rest of the explanation.

Do not convert every foreign figure automatically. Convert only when it improves
understanding.

### Dates and financial years

Use the source's reporting period precisely. Distinguish between calendar year 2025,
fiscal year FY2024–25, and quarter ended June 2025. Never compare a calendar-year
figure with a fiscal-year figure without stating the difference.

Use an en dash in date ranges, not a hyphen: FY2024–25. 2020–2025.

### Data language

Use:
- "about" for rounded figures.
- "roughly" for approximate quantities.
- "reported" when repeating a source's figure.
- "estimated" when the source labels it an estimate.
- "calculated" when the channel performs the calculation.
- "according to" when attribution is necessary.

## Rhetorical Devices

Rhetorical devices are allowed only when they clarify the argument or improve
spoken rhythm.

### Allowed

#### Rhetorical questions

Use when they introduce a genuine analytical problem.

Good:
> If the product was profitable, why did the company keep running out of cash?

Do not use merely for drama:
> But can Pakistan survive?

#### Parallel structure

Use to clarify a comparison:
> The state reduced the immediate bill, shifted the cost to lenders, and delayed
> the underlying reform.

Do not use as a slogan.

#### Contrast

Use when both sides are supported by evidence:
> The company was growing revenue, but its cash position was weakening.

#### Controlled repetition

Repeat a key term only when repetition helps the viewer remember the mechanism.
No more than two deliberate repetitions in a passage.

#### Analogy

Use only when it makes a technical mechanism easier to understand. State the actual
mechanism immediately after the analogy.

### Restricted (use sparingly)

- Metaphor.
- Irony.
- Direct address to the viewer.
- Deliberate sentence fragments.
- One-line paragraphs.

These must not replace evidence or explanation.

### Banned

Do not use:

- Hyperbole.
- False urgency.
- Emotional blackmail or moral panic.
- Absolute claims without proof.
- Hero-versus-villain framing.
- Fake suspense.
- Loaded rhetorical questions.
- Mockery or sarcasm directed at a person, group, institution, or region.
- Slogans presented as analysis.
- "Everyone knows" claims.
- "The truth is" as a substitute for evidence.
- "You will not believe" language.

## Attribution

Attribute information according to the strength and type of evidence.

### Directly verified primary source

Use when the claim comes from an official filing, law, budget, regulatory document,
company report, audited account, or official dataset.

Templates:
- "The company's FY2024 annual report shows..."
- "The State Bank of Pakistan reported..."
- "According to the Pakistan Bureau of Statistics..."
- "The Finance Division's budget documents show..."
- "The regulation requires..."

Do not say "according to reports" when the primary document is available.

### Named secondary source

Use when a reputable publication reports information the channel has not
independently verified through the primary document.

Templates:
- "Dawn Business reported in [month year] that..."
- "Business Recorder reported that..."
- "According to [publication], ..."

Use the publication's name and date where the timing matters.

### Reported allegation or disputed claim

Use precise attribution and do not state the claim as established fact.

Templates:
- "[Publication] reported that [person or institution] was alleged to have..."
- "The company denied the allegation."
- "The available record does not independently establish..."
- "This claim remains disputed."
- "The evidence supports [narrow finding], but not [stronger conclusion]."

Never write: The company stole money.

Write: Investigators alleged that funds were diverted. The company denied the
allegation, and the available documents do not establish the full claim. [SOURCE]

### Expert analysis

Templates:
- "[Name], [role], told [publication] that..."
- "[Name] argues that..."
- "In a report for [institution], [name] estimates..."
- "That is an interpretation, not a directly measured figure."

Do not present an expert's opinion as a fact.

### Channel analysis

Signal analysis clearly.

Templates:
- "The economic effect is straightforward: ..."
- "The comparison suggests..."
- "The more defensible interpretation is..."
- "This does not prove [strong claim]. It does show [narrow claim]."
- "The incentive created by this arrangement is..."

### Attribution placement

Place attribution close to the claim it supports.

Do not place one source tag at the end of a paragraph containing several unrelated
claims.

## Banned or Restricted Language

### Corporate and consulting clichés

Avoid unless used critically and precisely:

Game-changer. At the end of the day. Synergy. Disrupt. Disruption. Leverage (when
"use" works). Ecosystem (when "market" or "network" works). Stakeholders (when the
actual group can be named). Value proposition. Best-in-class. World-class. Scalable.
Robust. Holistic. Seamless. Cutting-edge. Innovative. Transformative. Strategic
imperative. Unlock. Unlock value. Drive growth. Deliver impact. Move the needle.
Low-hanging fruit. Win-win. Thought leadership. Mission-critical. Future-proof.
End-to-end. Going forward. At scale. In this space. In today's environment.
The new normal.

### Generic documentary filler

Avoid:

The shocking truth. The hidden truth. The untold story. What happened next changed
everything. This changes everything. The answer may surprise you. You will not believe.
Behind the scenes. The real story is. The truth is. Here is the thing. The question
is simple. It gets worse. It gets more complicated. This is where things get interesting.
But that is only half the story. And this is just the beginning. The numbers speak for
themselves. Let that sink in. Think about that. Read that again. Make no mistake.
At first glance. On the surface. Beneath the surface. More than meets the eye.
A perfect storm. A ticking time bomb. A house of cards. A race against time.
A crisis waiting to happen.

### Overstated analytical language

Avoid unless directly justified by evidence:

Destroyed. Crushed. Killed. Devastated. Weaponised. Rigged. Stolen. Captured.
Predatory. Reckless. Corrupt. Sham. Disastrous. Catastrophic. Unprecedented.
Massive. Huge. Explosive. Radical. Draconian. Needless.

Replace with the precise description: "Reduced." "Raised costs." "Transferred the
loss." "Created an incentive." "Limited competition." "The documents show."
"The evidence suggests."

Avoid as false certainty: Obviously. Clearly. Simply. Completely. Always. Never.

### Qualification

Do not mechanically remove a banned word when it is the technically correct term.
"Ecosystem" may be used when describing a network of firms, institutions, suppliers,
and customers. "Disruption" may be used when describing a documented structural break.
Define the term or replace it with plainer language when possible.

## Voice Test

Before accepting a paragraph, check:

1. Does it contain a concrete action, condition, decision, or consequence?
2. Does it explain why the fact matters?
3. Does it reveal a mechanism or incentive?
4. Does it sound natural when read aloud?
5. Does it use precise language instead of emotional adjectives?
6. Does it move the viewer toward the next question?
7. Could the paragraph appear unchanged in a research report? If yes, rewrite it
   for spoken narrative.

A paragraph should not merely tell the viewer what happened. It should help the
viewer understand why it happened and what changed because of it.
