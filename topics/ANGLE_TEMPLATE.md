## Broad subject

Pakistan Railways is a state-owned carrier that once moved the country's people and freight and now moves neither efficiently — the question is why, and whether it is recoverable.

---

## Approved angle

### Angle 1 — The Freight Betrayal

| Field | Content |
|-------|---------|
| Lens | Winners and losers · Hidden cost transfer · Policy versus ground reality |
| One-sentence framing | Pakistan Railways once moved roughly 12 million tonnes of freight a year and commanded over 70% of national freight — today it moves fewer than 9 million tonnes and holds under 5% — and that collapse is not a market outcome but a policy outcome that every Pakistani pays for in higher prices. |
| Central question | Why did Pakistan Railways effectively exit the freight business, and what has that cost the economy? |
| Tentative thesis | The collapse of rail freight was the product of highway investment decisions, political management of an institution that could not prioritise commercial freight, and an entrenched road-transport economy that now makes the reversal structurally difficult. |
| Main entities and institutions | Pakistan Railways (Ministry of Railways), National Highway Authority, Planning Commission, trucking associations, Ministry of Finance, World Bank, ADB |
| Evidence required | Ministry of Railways freight tonnage data by year (1980–2024); NHA capex by decade; cost-per-tonne-km rail vs. road in Pakistan; current PR freight revenue as share of total revenue; World Bank / ADB transport assessments |
| Pakistani economic relevance | Rail freight costs roughly 3–4× less per tonne-km than road; the gap is paid by manufacturers, farmers, and consumers in every price they pay |
| Audience consequence | Logistics businesses, manufacturers, and investors should understand Pakistan's structural freight cost disadvantage and why it is not easily reversible |
| Deliberate exclusions | ML-1 financing details; passenger service; land assets; safety record |
| Main weakness / research risk | Rail freight may have declined partly for legitimate market reasons (trucking flexibility); counterargument must be addressed honestly |

# Episode 11 — Pakistan Steel Mills

## Episode metadata

- **Episode number:** 11
- **Topic:** Pakistan Steel Mills (PSM)
- **Why now:** PSM recorded Rs 245.9 billion in accumulated losses per June 2026 AGP audit; the government has attempted closure or privatisation multiple times without resolution; ongoing FX cost of steel imports makes the dormant mill a live policy question.
- **Created:** 2026-08-22

---

## Broad subject

Pakistan Steel Mills is a state-owned integrated steel facility at Bin Qasim, Karachi that stopped producing in June 2015. It has not produced steel since. It continues to employ several thousand people, service debt, and draw government support — at a cost the AGP estimates at Rs 245.9 billion in accumulated losses over 15 years.

---

## Approved angle

### PSM: The SOE That Ate Itself

| Field | Content |
|-------|---------|
| Lens | Institutional failure · Incentive conflict · Decision-focused · Policy versus ground reality |
| One-sentence framing | Pakistan Steel Mills failed not because of a single act of corruption, but because a sequence of individually defensible choices — by governments, courts, unions, and lenders — removed every mechanism that could have forced correction. |
| Central question | How did Pakistan's only integrated steel mill become a facility that costs billions without producing anything? |
| Tentative thesis | Each institutional actor (government, courts, unions, lenders) made a locally rational decision that collectively locked the mill in a suspended state more expensive than any available resolution. |
| Main entities | PSM management, Ministry of Industries, Ministry of Finance, SSGC, steel unions, Supreme Court, AGP, commercial lenders, private re-rolling sector, construction sector buyers |
| Evidence required | PSM production history and shutdown date; accumulated losses by year; government loans and payroll support; Supreme Court privatisation ruling (2006); workforce data; Pakistan steel import volume and value; cost per tonne vs. market price; 2020 retrenchment outcome; 2023 closure attempt |
| Pakistani economic relevance | Pakistan imports >$3 billion in steel annually, pays carrying costs on a non-producing mill, and has no domestic benchmark price — every construction project absorbs the global commodity cycle without a domestic alternative |
| Audience consequence | Manufacturers, investors, construction sector, and policy-focused viewers should understand the structural cost of SOE paralysis and why the "just privatise it" argument is harder than it sounds |
| Deliberate exclusions | Corruption allegations against specific individuals; steel sector re-rollers' internal economics; international steel market dynamics beyond their impact on PSM |
| Main weakness | Some government carrying-cost figures are not consolidated in one public document; current headcount post-2020 retrenchments is unclear |

# Episode 12 — Public Transport (Metro Bus / BRT)

## Episode metadata

- **Episode number:** 12
- **Topic:** Public Transport — Pakistan's Metro Bus / BRT systems
- **Why now:** Multiple provinces continue expanding flagship BRT/Metro corridors (Lahore, Islamabad, Peshawar, with Karachi's Green Line now operational) while carrying undisclosed or under-discussed operating subsidies — a live, ongoing fiscal commitment rather than a historical one.
- **Created:** 2026-08-22

---

## Broad subject

Public transport in Pakistan spans a fractured system — collapsed municipal bus networks, high-profile Metro Bus/BRT corridors, and a large informal sector that fills the gaps the state doesn't. This episode isolates one mechanism: the fiscal economics of the flagship Metro Bus/BRT systems specifically.

---

## Approved angle

### Angle 2 — The Metro Bus Subsidy Machine

| Field | Content |
|-------|---------|
| Lens | Hidden subsidy / cost transfer · Institutional incentive conflict · Policy versus ground reality |
| One-sentence framing | Pakistan's flagship Metro Bus/BRT systems deliver a highly visible, popular launch, but the fare is set below the true cost of operation from day one — turning a celebrated infrastructure launch into a compounding, largely invisible fiscal liability. |
| Central question | Do Pakistan's flagship Metro Bus/BRT systems (Lahore, Islamabad, Peshawar) solve urban mobility, or are they subsidy commitments governments underprice at launch and cannot sustain? |
| Tentative thesis | The same underpricing-becomes-permanent-liability mechanism already documented in PSM and PIA applies to urban transit: each system's fare was set low enough to be popular at launch, not high enough to cover its running cost, and the resulting subsidy gap compounds rather than shrinks over time. |
| Main entities and institutions | Punjab Metro Bus Authority, KP BRT (TransPeshawar), Islamabad Metro (CDA), Sindh/Karachi Green Line administration, provincial finance departments, provincial transport departments |
| Evidence required | Farebox recovery ratio and annual subsidy by system; capital cost at launch vs. actual; current ridership by system; an internationally-recognised farebox-recovery benchmark for context |
| Pakistani economic relevance | Multiple provinces are actively expanding or proposing new BRT/Metro corridors — the fiscal pattern established by existing systems directly shapes what taxpayers are committing to going forward |
| Audience consequence | Policy-focused viewers, provincial-budget watchers, and infrastructure investors should be able to read a "successful" flagship transit project's actual unit economics rather than its ribbon-cutting narrative |
| Deliberate exclusions | Informal transport sector (Chingchi, vans); Karachi's pre-BRT transit vacuum; intercity coach competition with Pakistan Railways; women's mobility/access; road safety; engineering/construction quality of the corridors themselves |
| Main weakness / research risk | Public transit is subsidised almost everywhere in the world — a low farebox recovery ratio alone isn't damning. The script must engage this directly rather than treat "subsidised" as inherently a failure. Provincial transport-authority budget documents may also be difficult to access in consolidated form. |
