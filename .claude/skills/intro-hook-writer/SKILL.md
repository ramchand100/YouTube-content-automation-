# Intro Hook Writer

Drafts the opening 30-40 seconds of a long-form YouTube video. This is the
highest-leverage part of any script — it's what decides whether a video
gets 100 views or 100K. The framework below comes from breaking down what
actually happens in the first seconds of real viral intros.

This skill is used by many different creators across many different
channels, niches, languages, and tones. Never assume a specific channel
identity, brand voice, or language by default — match whatever the current
user's channel actually sounds like.

## The 5 Steps (internal framework — don't label these in the output)

**Step 1 — Meet Expectations.**
The title + thumbnail create a promise in the viewer's mind before they even
click. The first line(s) of the intro must immediately confirm that promise
— show/say the thing they clicked for, right away. This doesn't mean
repeating the title word for word; it means the opening beat has to feel
like "yes, this is what I came for." This step effectively always comes
first — it's the anchor everything else builds on.

**Step 2 — Create Context.**
One or two sentences on why this topic matters / what problem it solves for
the viewer. Not an exhaustive explanation — just enough for the viewer to
understand the stakes or relevance before you go further. Overexplaining
here kills the rest of the video and bloats the intro.

**Step 3 — Create Curiosity.**
The most important step. Plant a question in the viewer's mind that only
watching the full video answers. Common techniques:
- Promise a specific number of steps/points but don't reveal them ("5 steps," "3 reasons") — the count itself is the tease.
- Name a framework/method/label without explaining it yet (e.g. "the GPS method") — the unexplained name is the hook.
- Reference the best moment from later in the video, then cut away right before the payoff.
- More curiosity gaps in the intro = stronger hook, but don't force it if it doesn't feel natural.

**Step 4 — Add a Differentiator.**
Answer the unspoken question: "why should I watch YOUR video on this topic
and not someone else's?" Pick whichever angle fits the creator and topic:
- Authority/experience ("I've been doing this for X years")
- Personal results/credibility stat tied to the topic
- Effort/investment signal (how much time, research, or money went into this video)
- Unique data, research depth, or a specific real example no one else has

**Step 5 — Sound Design.**
Not scripted text — a production note. Background music and sound effects
during the intro should match the mood/vibe of the topic (e.g. suspense for
a problem-focused topic, upbeat/energetic for a growth win). Mention this
as a one-line aside after the script, don't try to write it into the
script itself.

## Rules for combining the steps

- Step 1 goes first. Steps 2, 3, and 4 can happen in **any order** —
  whatever connects most naturally into a flowing story for that specific
  topic and creator.
- Not every intro needs all 5 steps every time. Use the ones that make the
  intro strongest for this topic — don't force a step in just to check a box.
- Never overpromise in the intro. If a bold claim is made, the rest of the
  video needs to actually deliver on it.
- If stuck, use the "imagine you're the viewer" test: what would make
  someone stop scrolling and get hooked if they saw this exact video appear?

## Workflow

1. **Get the topic/title.** If the user only gives a rough topic with no
   working title, draft the intro around the topic directly — don't block
   on demanding a polished title first.
2. **Check if this needs current information.** If the topic touches
   current affairs, a controversy, breaking news, a recent event, or
   anything where facts could have changed or gone stale (a stat, a
   figure, "what happened," a recent announcement, an ongoing situation)
   — search the web first and ground the intro in accurate, up-to-date
   details before writing a single line. Don't rely on memory for this
   category; a wrong or outdated fact in the opening line undermines the
   whole video. For topics with no time-sensitive element (how-to,
   personal story, general educational, challenge/entertainment), skip
   the search.
3. **Use any video content the user has already given you.** If the user
   describes what the video actually covers — specific points, the
   structure, a key twist, results, data, a specific example — treat that
   as the real material to build the intro from, not just the bare topic.
   The strongest intros are pulled directly from what's actually in the
   video (a real number, a real turn of events, a real promise) rather
   than a generic version of the topic. Mine what they've given you for
   the strongest possible opening line, context, curiosity gap, and
   differentiator.
4. **Match the creator's voice.** Default to writing the script in
   **Hinglish**, conversational and confident, no filler. Only switch to
   plain English if either of these is true:
   - The user's Project instructions / channel context explicitly state
     that their channel language is English, or
   - The user explicitly tells you (in this conversation) to write in
     English.
   If there's other tone/style signal available (existing scripts to
   reference, a stated brand voice, formal vs casual), follow that too —
   language defaults to Hinglish, everything else adapts to the specific
   creator.
5. **Identify the promise.** Work out what a title + thumbnail for this
   topic would lead a viewer to expect. This defines the opening beat.
6. **Draft Context, Curiosity, and a Differentiator** in whatever order
   best fits this specific topic — weave them into one natural, connected
   mini-story, not a checklist recited in order.
7. **Target length: ~125 words (30-40 seconds spoken).** This is a hard
   target, not a suggestion — compress rather than leaving in a weak line.
8. **Deliver the finished intro as a single, flowing block of readable
   script** — the way it would actually be read on camera or by a
   voiceover. Do NOT label sentences with "Step 1," "Context," "Hook," etc.
   in the output. The steps are your internal checklist, not something the
   viewer or reader of the script should see.
9. After the script, add a short one-line sound design suggestion (Step 5)
   as a separate aside, clearly not part of the script itself.
10. If asked for a breakdown or explanation of why a line works, give it —
    but only when explicitly requested, and keep it separate from the
    script itself.

## Quick reference: how this framework shows up in real intros

- Title/thumbnail promise gets confirmed in the first shot or first line.
- A short line on stakes/relevance follows ("why does this even matter").
- A specific number, unnamed method, or teased payoff creates the curiosity
  gap that keeps someone watching.
- A credibility or effort signal (experience, results, cost, time invested)
  answers "why trust this creator on this topic."
- The order of context, curiosity, and differentiator varies video to
  video — there's no fixed template, only a natural flow.
