## Part 1 — The Port Built for Cargo That Never Came

In 2024, / Karachi Port moved more cargo in a **single day** / than Gwadar moved in the **entire year.**

*(let that sit — pause about a full second before continuing)*

That same year, / in December, / a government official stood up in Pakistan's National Assembly. He said Gwadar port was **fully operational.** He said it was ready to handle **any kind of cargo.**

*(flat, neutral delivery — you are reporting what was said, not reacting to it; no sarcasm in the voice)*

So which one is true?

*(genuine question, slower, real pause after it)*

In this episode, / we look at both sides of that gap. What Gwadar was **actually** promised. Why the cargo **never came.** And what it costs the rest of Pakistan, / that one of its biggest port projects still barely moves anything at all.

*(normal pace resumes here — this is the roadmap, keep it brisk and clear, not dramatic)*

Start with the numbers everyone agrees on. Gwadar's 34,000 tonnes in 2024 was already a drop. The year before, in 2023, the port had moved **592,000 tonnes.** Since the port opened, it has handled a cumulative total of **4,789 containers.** The port was built to handle **240,000 containers** every single year.

*(slower on the two big numbers — 592,000 and 240,000 — give the viewer time to register the gap)*

Picture a warehouse built with a hundred rooms, / all empty, / waiting to be filled. Gwadar has used about **two** of them. Not two this year. **Two, total,** / since the day it opened.

*(slow down through this whole paragraph — this is the clearest image in the episode, don't rush it)*

Now look at the other side of Karachi. Port Qasim, right next to Karachi Port, handled **45.3 million tonnes** that same year. Between Karachi Port and Port Qasim, these two ports move something close to **90 percent** of everything Pakistan ships in or out by sea.

Picture it this way. If Karachi Port and Port Qasim's cargo for that year were a small **mountain,** / Gwadar's cargo would be a single **brick** / sitting at the bottom of it. Gwadar is not simply a smaller port than its neighbours. By the numbers, / it has **barely started.**

*(slower again on the mountain/brick line — second big image of the section)*

Gwadar was never meant to be that brick. It was designed and promoted as Pakistan's third option, a deep sea port built so the country did not have to send almost all of its trade through one stretch of coastline near Karachi. Billions of dollars went into building it. So why did the cargo never follow the money?

*(pick pace back up slightly through the middle sentences, then slow again on the closing question — end-of-part questions should always land, never trail off)*

## Part 2 — What Was Promised

*(overall: this part is explanatory, not dramatic — keep it closer to normal conversational pace throughout, reserve the slow treatment for Part 1's numbers and Part 3's mechanism)*

Start with why Gwadar was picked in the first place. It sits on the Arabian Sea, about **400 kilometres** outside the Strait of Hormuz. That strait is a narrow passage. Close to a **fifth** of the world's oil and gas passes through it. For ships moving goods between China and the Middle East, Africa, or Central Asia, a route through Gwadar can be **shorter** than the usual path through the Strait of Malacca. A shorter route means real savings in time and fuel. On paper, / the location made sense.

*(slight emphasis on "on paper" — it's doing real work, foreshadowing the turn later)*

Gwadar's story as a modern port begins in February 2013. That is when Pakistan handed the port's second phase of construction to a Chinese state owned company, China Overseas Port Holding Company. That was just one piece of a much bigger commitment. Investment pledges under the China Pakistan Economic Corridor started at around **46 billion dollars.** They eventually grew to around **62 billion dollars.** At the time, / that was roughly a **fifth of Pakistan's entire economy,** / one rupee out of every five the country produced in a year.

*(slower on "one rupee out of every five" — that's the relatable version of the number, give it room)*

Out of that huge pledge, the port company itself put in around **1.62 billion dollars** to build Gwadar's deep water facilities, / a small slice, / **well under 3 percent,** / of the full 62 billion dollar plan. By 2016, the port had three berths. It could formally handle **137,000 containers** a year. That milestone came a full decade after the first phase of construction had already finished.

Around the port, the government set up the Gwadar Free Zone. Inside this zone, new businesses get lower taxes and fewer rules on moving goods in and out. The idea is simple. A factory that brings in raw material, turns it into a finished product, and ships that product back out should not have to pay full tax twice, once coming in and once going out. Waiving that cost is meant to pull in manufacturers who would otherwise build their factory somewhere else. A company can join for as little as **500,000 dollars** plus a registration fee, / roughly **139 million rupees** at today's exchange rate. That is well beyond what almost any small or medium Pakistani business could put down. Several dozen businesses have registered there over the years. Only a handful are reported as **fully up and running.**

*(land "fully up and running" with a touch of weight — it's the quiet undercut at the end of an otherwise upbeat paragraph)*

This was the full case for Gwadar. A deep water port. Built with serious money. Backed by a tax free zone. Connected to the rest of the country by new roads, and eventually a railway. The plan was to give Pakistan a second major gateway to the sea, and give Balochistan a real share in the growth that gateway would bring.

*(this list sentence — "A deep water port. Built with serious money..." — reads well as short, near-equal beats, almost like ticking off a checklist)*

That was the plan **on paper.** So what actually happened / once the port opened?

*(pause before "once the port opened" — this is the turn into Part 3, let it breathe)*

## Part 3 — Why the Cargo Didn't Follow

*(overall: this is the mechanism part — slow and clear, especially the insurance explanation, which is the most important paragraph to land well in the whole episode)*

In 2017, a year after Gwadar had formally opened, the port received **145 containers.** All of that was project cargo, materials for building the port itself. **Not one** general commercial cargo ship arrived that year. That is close to no trade at all, for a facility built to handle well over a hundred thousand containers a year.

*(slow, flat delivery on "not one" — let the absence of the number carry the weight, no need to editorialize with tone)*

Part of the answer is simple. The port itself was finished. The country around it was **not.** A ship can dock at Gwadar. But the roads and the railway that would let cargo actually move in and out of the country were never built at the same pace as the berths.

Take the main road first. The M8 motorway was planned to run nearly **900 kilometres,** connecting Gwadar to Ratodero in Sindh, / roughly the same distance as driving from Karachi to Multan. A 193 kilometre stretch out of Gwadar opened in 2016. Large parts of the rest are **still unfinished today,** even though planning for this road goes back close to **two decades.** Now take the railway. A direct rail line connecting Gwadar to the national network has **not been built at all.** It is currently listed as a mid term project. It is expected to start in 2025 and finish by 2030. In plain terms, / a port promoted as Pakistan's future gateway / still has no working train line to the rest of the country.

*(slow down for the final sentence of this paragraph — it's the clearest summary line in Part 3)*

A report prepared for the Prime Minister looked into why cargo has stayed away. It pointed to several reasons. High costs of running the port. A lack of supporting infrastructure. Limited interest from international shipping companies. It also pointed to security concerns, serious enough that global insurance companies have been reluctant to cover cargo passing through the port.

*(the four-item list here can move at a slightly quicker, clipped pace — each phrase is its own short beat)*

That last point matters more than it might sound.

*(pause — direct address to the viewer, slower, like you're about to explain something worth paying attention to)*

Before a shipping company sends a ship anywhere, it needs **insurance,** for the ship and for the cargo. If insurance companies see a route as **risky,** they charge more for that cover. Sometimes they refuse to offer it at all. Either way, / the shipping company's cost of using that route goes **up.** And a route that costs more / gets chosen **less.** A handful of insurance decisions, made far away from Gwadar, / can keep cargo away just as effectively as a missing road.

*(this whole paragraph: slow, deliberate, one idea per breath — this is the mechanism explanation the episode depends on; do not rush it)*

None of this means building a port at Gwadar was the wrong call. Deep water access on that stretch of coast is real. The location does sit close to shipping lanes that matter for the wider region. What the evidence actually shows is narrower than that. A port needs a few basic things to move real cargo. Roads that work end to end. A working railway. Lower costs. Enough trust from shipping companies to send ships there. All of that was promised **long before** any of it was delivered. A berth without a finished road leading to it / is a berth that ships have very little reason to use.

*(the four-item list again reads as short clipped beats; slow back down for the final sentence)*

So if Gwadar has barely moved any cargo, / what does that actually cost the rest of the country?

*(slower, real question, pause after)*

## Part 4 — What Concentration Costs, and Whether Gwadar Can Still Matter

Picture almost everything Pakistan buys and sells by sea / passing through a **single gate.** Machinery for factories. Fuel for power plants and cars. Textiles and rice heading out to buyers overseas. That gate is not one port. It is **two,** sitting close together, on one stretch of Karachi's coastline. Nearly all of the country's trade by sea depends on them.

*(slow through the opening image, list of goods can move a little quicker — machinery, fuel, textiles — each a quick concrete beat)*

That alone is not a crisis. But it is a **concentration.** And any concentration carries a **cost.** If something disrupts either port, / bad weather, / a labour dispute, / heavy congestion, / there is no working alternative to absorb that trade. A third working port nearby would not remove that risk completely. But it would give the country somewhere else to send at least some of that cargo, if the other two ports go down at the same time. Gwadar was supposed to be that somewhere else. For most of its history, / it has not been able to play that role.

To be fair, / 2026 has looked different.

*(genuine shift in tone here — this is the episode being fair to the counter-evidence, let the pace lift slightly, sound like you mean it)*

In April alone, Gwadar processed around **11,000 containers,** more cargo than it handled in the whole of 2025. Over a three month stretch, the port moved roughly **200,000 tonnes** of cargo passing through on its way to other destinations. That sounds like a lot, / until you compare it to Port Qasim, / which moves that much cargo in about a **day and a half.** Still, it is **real growth.** It deserves to be said plainly, not brushed aside.

*(slow down on "day and a half" — that's the number doing the honest-complication work)*

But it also deserves a closer look.

*(pace shift back to investigative mode)*

Reporting on that growth ties much of it to trouble in the Strait of Hormuz, the narrow passage most Gulf shipping normally relies on. When that route gets less reliable, some cargo gets redirected to nearby ports. Gwadar is one of them. That is a real increase in traffic. It is **not the same thing** as Pakistan finally finishing the roads, the railway, and the cost problems the original plan depended on solving. If shipping through Hormuz goes back to normal, / there is no evidence yet / that this cargo stays at Gwadar.

*(slow, careful delivery on the last sentence — this is the honest hedge, it needs to sound considered, not dismissive)*

So what is the honest answer here? Why hasn't Gwadar become what it was promised to be?

*(both questions genuine, slower pace, real pauses after each)*

It is not one single failure, or one single decision. A few things actually determine whether a port gets used. Finished roads. A working railway. Manageable costs. Enough trust from shipping companies to send cargo there routinely. Every one of those was treated like a **detail** that could follow the port's opening. **Not one** of them was treated like a **requirement** that had to come first. Karachi Port and Port Qasim did not beat Gwadar because they are better ports. They beat Gwadar because they were **already connected.** They were **already trusted.** And they were **already carrying** the trade Gwadar was meant to share.

*(the three "already" phrases at the end should land with matching weight and rhythm — this is the thesis statement of the whole episode)*

Whether that changes now comes down to roads still being built, and a railway still years from finished, / not to the port itself. What would actually need to happen is specific, not vague. The rest of the M8 motorway would need to open. The rail link would need to move from a plan on paper to a finished line. And shipping companies and insurers would need enough confidence in the route to choose it on their own, without a crisis in the Gulf forcing their hand. None of that is impossible. **None of it has happened yet either.**

*(slow and level through the final two sentences — no rising drama, just a plain, weighted statement of fact)*

Until it does, / Pakistan's trade by sea will keep moving through the same stretch of Karachi coastline it always has. / And the port built to change that / will keep waiting for the cargo it was designed to carry.

*(slowest delivery of the whole episode on this final line — full stop pauses at each `/`, let the last sentence be the last thing the viewer hears clearly before it ends)*
